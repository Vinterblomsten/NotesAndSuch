Transcription regulators (Protein-DNA interaction)

Proteins can act upon and regulate the DNA transcription directly. There are many of these transcription regulators, many of which are transcription factors (but not all).

During transcription the general transcription factors (Or Sigma factor for prokaryotes) bind to the (core) promoters, binding RNA polymerase and initializes transcription. In the upstream region of the promoters, there are regulatory promotor regions, where "ligangs?" can bind to activate or repress the general transcription factors.

The transcription factors interact with the major groove of the DNA, as the chemical information is more accessible. Here some chemical groups in the nucleotide bases are exposed. This allows for

  • Hydrogen bonds
  • Hydrophobic interactions
  • Electrostatic interactions

The different base pairs this provide different binding possibilites:

The transcription factors usually interact with the DNA in the form of -helices forming some of the structures

  • Homeodomain
  • Zinc fingers
  • Leucine-zipper

Per -helix we can only bond to 2 or 3 base pairs, thus multiple -helices are usually needed to interact with the DNA.

The transcription factors can bind in different places on the regulatory promoter region, having different effects on the transcription- all dependent on the genes and promotor regions them selves.

Prokaryotic Transcription Regulation

Bacteria have a small genomes of merely 4 million bp. With only 4000 proteins, and 60% active at any time, they need to be very adapative to the environment in terms of gene expression.

The genes in the bacteria are often arranged in OPERONs, in which multiple genes are expressed by the same promotor (and promotor region) - The result can be simultaneous synthesis of enzymes that act in the same pathway, called coordinated expression. This coordinated expression of the OPERON are controlled by a DNA segment called the operator.

  • The operator can be activating / repressing dependent on the transcription factor bound to the operator.

Example: The E.coli tryptophan operon - a binary switch

  • When tryptophane is high, the tryptophane binds to the repressor, which is activated and binds to the operator, restricting the transcription
  • When it is low, the repressor is inactivated, and releases the operator, allowing the RNA polymerase to transcribe the gene.

Epigenetics

While every cell in the body contains the same genome, we find that of the 20.000 protein-coding genes, only 50-60% are expressed

  • 8-9k are shared between all cells
  • 2-3k

By definition epigenetics refers to heritable, reversable changes in the gene functions that don't change the DNA sequence. The epigenome thus sits "on top" of the genome - a collection of the compounds and modifications to the chromatin that regulates gene expression without changing the underlying DNA sequence.

The epicgenetic mechanisms are multiple

  • Histone modifications
  • DNA methylation
  • Chromatin remodeling
  • 3D genome organization
  • Non-coding RNAs

The histone modifications ...

The DNA methylation is when the DNA methyltransferase (DNMT) ads a methyl group to the cytosine. TET enzymes does the opposite, removing the methyl group

  • It only happens on the CpG sequences in the DNA.

The DNA methylation happens all over, but it is especially important on promoters, where methylation blocks the transcription factors, effectively turning the gene off.

Post-Transcriptional Modifications

There is multiple steps of Post-Transcriptional Modifications, but our focus is on

  • RNA PRocessing control
  • mRNA degradation control
  • Translation control

After the transciription we have the pre-mRNA, which needs processing

  • Splicing
  • Capping
  • Polyadenylation

The mature mRNA also includes untranslated regions (UTRs) before the start codon, and after the stop codon. These play an important role in translocation, etc.

This gives os room for regulatory intervention. The UTRs include the 5' UTR before the start codon, and the 3' UTR after the stop codon.

When translation begins, the small ribosome subunit binds to the mRNA cap, and searches for the start codon.

  • RNA-binding proteins in the 5' UTR can block this search, and thus inhibit the translation
  • Similar stem loops (hairpins) on the 5' UTR can slow down or inhibit the translation

Non-coding RNAs

Some types of RNA are used for translation (tRNA and rRNA). Some however regulates other types of RNA:

  • Micro RNA (miRNA)
  • Small interfering RNAs (siRNA)
  • Long Non-coding RNAs (IncRNA)

miRNA
The most important regulatory RNA is the miRNA. We have about 2000 miRNA genes, and about 60% of human protein-coding genes are regulated by miRNAs.

  • One miRNA can regulate hundreds of targets
  • One mRNA contains mulitple miRNA binding sites

Usually the binding takes place in the 3' UTR, but sometimes at CDS and rarely at 5' UTR. They primarily act as

The precursor miRNA (pri-miRNA) is transcribed like other genes by teh RNA polymerase II. It is then processed into short intermediates (pre-miRNA). This is loaded into the RISC complex.

  • Scanning for complementary target
    • Good mach -> mRNA is degradaded by nucleases within RISC
    • Not as good a muach -> Translation reduced, mRNA sequestered and eventually degredaded by nucleases in the cytosol

An example is the miR-15a/miR-16-1 in B-cells, which inhibits the BCL2 gene (anti apoptopic proteins), resulting in more apoptosis

  • If the microRNA is removed (leuchemia), the BCL2 gene is not inhibited, and the apoptosis is blocked

siRNA
These are used in plants and "lower" animals as a defence mechanism, and are also used in reasearch to create gene knock-down effects (functional analysis).

  • It can also be used in drugs

During virus infection, cells are infected by foreign RNA. This is cleaved by DICER into double- stranded siRNAs. These form complexes with RISC proteins, which can degrade mRNA just as with miRNA.
