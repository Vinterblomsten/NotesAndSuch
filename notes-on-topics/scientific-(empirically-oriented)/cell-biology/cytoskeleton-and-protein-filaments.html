Cytoskeleton and Protein Filaments

Tags: #CellBiology

The cytoskeleton gives the cell form and mobility. It consists of the three classes of filaments

  • Actine (diam. 7 nm)
  • Intermediaru (diam. 10 nm)
  • Microtubili (diam. 25 nm)

Intermediary filament

They are formed by -helical peptide chains forming coiled-coil dimers, these are staggered to form tetramers, which can be packed head to head in "layers" of eight

  • This means that they are not polar

    This rope-like structure gives them tensile strength - that is ability to take tension without breaking.

Except for lamin, which exist in all nuclei of animal cells, the peptides are specific for each tissue-type

  • Keratin filaments play an important role in epthelial cells

Desmosomes are the contact point between intermediary filaments of neighbouring cells. (See Cell-Cell and Cell-ECM Contact)

Actine filament (F-actine)

Actine have a variety of functions, but generally it is associated with the movements of the cell - especially those related to the membrane. But also structure and intercellular contact (Tight junctions).

Many of the functions are done with the help of actin-binding proteins. Some of which include:

  • Nucleating protein
  • Monomer-sequestering protein
  • Severing protein - for breaking filaments into smaller parts
  • Bundling proteins
  • Myosin motor proteins - Different types!
  • Capping proteins
  • Cross-linking proteins

Actin filament is on its own (no actin-binding proteins) an unstable structure, that can assemble and disassemble spontaneously. The actine monomers are called G-actine. It is a polar molecules meaning, that the direction matters, and it will always bind its minus-end, to the exposed plus-end in the sequence, called the F-actine.

The G-actine comes together in a polar filament with a coupled ATP. Shortly after incorporation however, the ATP is hydrolyzed, and the binding is not as strong. Thus the ADP-actin dissociates in the minus end (if no protein stop it). This means that the f-actin grows and shrinks, depending of the rates polymerization and depolymerization. When these are equal, we say that the actin is treadmilling.

The critical concentration of G-actine - is the tipping point for polymerization and depolymerization:

  • : Polymerization

Some proteins can catalyze the synthesis of F-actine.

  • Formins cap the minus end
  • Spires
  • ARP2/3 complex

Structural functions af Actin

The actin is responsible for cell surface specializations such as microvilli found on the apical surface of some epithelial cells.

It also plays a role in holding the plasma membrane in shape via the cortex, and the structure of adhesion belts in epithelial cells.

Actine dynamics

F-actine affects not just the structure, but also the dynamics of the plasma membrane and the cell it self.

Some bacteria can push forward by polymerization of tail f-actines - called actin filament comet

Cell migration is movement of the human cells caused by actine

  • The actine creates lamellipodium by pushing the plasmamembrane.
  • Filopodia are stiff thin protrutions, which are produced simmilarly - this is how the axon of the neurons are produced.
  • The actin polymerization begins by EGF signalling
  • This activates cofilin - severing the capped end of the f-actine by the membrane - exposing the plus end
  • Thus the polymerization begins of the exposed plus ends (ATP F-actin caps)
    • Arp2/3 fascilitates dendritic nucleation, that is the branching of the actine to cover more area forming lamellipodium
    • Formin promotes the polymerization of long straigth filaments forming filopedia

When in the new region, the filopedia of the leading edge of the lamellipodium and the lamellipodium it self then adhere to molecules in the extracellular matrix or on the neighboring cell surface via integrins in the plasma membrane of the migrating cell.

  • The cell then contracts by myosin-1 sliding the stress fibers forward in the "end" of the cell - pulling the back of the cell body forward

Myosin works together with f-actine to create movement

  • Myosin 1 - motor proteins that can transport cargo along the filmanet
  • Myosin 2 - the proteins responsible for muscle cell contration

Myosin 1 has a single globular head domain, that binds to actin filament and hydrolyzes ATP to allow directional movement. - The actual movement dynamic is a bit unclear

  • They can transport cargo along actine filaments
  • They can be bound to plasma membrane, and thus pull the cell along the actin.
  • It moves unidirectionally towards to plus end of the filament

Actine regulation - The f-actine in the cytoskeleton is controlled by GTPaser
An examples is:

  • VEGF binds to VEGF-receptor extracellularly
  • The GTPase Rac1 is activated
  • This activatres the kinase PAK
  • Which activates the kinase LIMK
  • This inhibits cofilin, which means that the ADP-actin filament is not severed, and the network will stabilize (decrease new polymerization)
    This is because the severing of ADP-actin filaments will stimulate new polymerizations.

Actin also plays an important role in the mitosis, that is the cytokinesis. Here actin creates a contractile ring including:

  • tropomyosin
  • myosin II
  • formin
  • crosslinking proteins
    This ring will tighten and "pinch" the cell in two. (The last part is carried out by ESCRT-III complex)

Microtubuli

Microtubi are organised from the microtubli organization center (MTOC), which is either centrosomes or basal bodies

Formed by dimer of -tubulin and -tubilin

  • Polar with -tubilin in the plus end and -tubilin in the minus end
  • They form long a cylinder of 13 protofilaments in a ring

Most microtubilu extend from centrosomes. These constists of a pair of centrioles (consisting of short microubili fibers) and protein complex with -tubilin rings, which are perfect starting points for forming microtubili, as the energy requirement for spontaneous cyllinders forming is too big.

  • They grow their plus ends outwards from the -tubulin rings

Dynamic instability
Like actine - we use energy by bindig tubuline dimers with GTP. This GTP is hydrolyzed once part of the microubuli. When phosphorylzed, the dimers binds strongly, whereas dephosphorylized dimers binds weakly, and will tear away from the microtobuli, if exposed to an open plus end.

  • Thus when the addition of dimers is faster than the hydrolyzis, the microtubuli grows - Elongation
  • If it is slower, the dimers peel away - Rapid shortening


Each of these microtubili spontaneously grows and shrinks independent of each other, sometimes disappearing completely, until the plus end finds some capping protein (or are associated with other proteions), in which case it is stable.

Cilias

Microtubuli form the cilia on the epithel cells. These hairlike structure extends from the basal body. These consists of two central microtubuli surrounded by nine pair of microtubuli and some structural proteins.

Most importantly though, is the motor protein ciliary dynein (Different from transport dynein). These motor proteins slides the pairs of microtubulis away from each other. Linking proteins keeps the slide from being even, and instead by tension, creates the bending of the cilia.

Cellular transport the polarity of the microtubuli makes both anterograde and retrograde transport possible (Anterograde transport means away from the MCOT / soma)

  • The motor protein dynein transports cargo retrograde
    • Attachment proteins determine cargo type
  • The motor protein kinesen (related to myosin)
    • It can have attachment proteins, but doesn't always

The directional movement along the microtubuli is called saltatory movement. Both protein have many conformations, that if left at random, would make them "walk" back and forth in no direction on average, what we call brownian movement or random walk. By using energy from ATP-hydrolysis to drive the series of conformations, they form directional movement. They do vary somewhat in the mechanism:

  • The conformational changes of kinesin makes it "walk" along the fiber, putting one globular head in front of the other again and again.
  • The conformation changes of dynein makes it "inch" along the fiber, moving the heads small steps, without flipping each step.

The transport along the micro tubuli in the axons illustrate the velocity of transports.

  • The anterograde transport can vary in speed depending on transport, but as fast as 400 mm/day.
    • This could be synaptic vesicles, enzymes and neurotransmitters (200-400 mm/day)
    • Mitochondria (50-100 mm/day)
    • Slow transport with Slow Component a (SCa) (0.1 - 1 mm/day) and Slow Component b (SCb) (2-6 mm/day) - Cytoskeleton proteins, clathrines, and cytoplasmic enzymes
  • The retrograde transport is more constant in speed at 100-200 mm/day.
    • Prelysosomal vesicles, recycled proteins, neurothropic viruses, etc.

The ER is attached to kinesine which pulls it outwards into the cytoplasm, while the golgi is attached to dynein pulling it towards the nucleus (and the ER)

The microtubuli doesn't reach the membrane, so in order to go all the way, the actine filaments must also be used for transport

  • The kinsesin can pass along its cargo from microtubili transport to actine transport by binding it to actine motor proteins and releasing it it self.
