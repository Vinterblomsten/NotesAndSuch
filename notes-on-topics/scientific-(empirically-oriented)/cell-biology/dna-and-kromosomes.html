Compositions and Structure

The nitrogen containing bases that are the fundamental part of the genome sequence, is divided into pyramidines and purines.

Dexoyribonucleic Acid (DNA) consists of two chains of nucleotides (not to be confused with nucleosides).

  • In DNA the nucleotides consists of a deoxyribose bound to the given base by a -glycoside bond with one to three phosphates bound to the 5-carbon
  • In RNA the it is ribose instead of deoxyribose - Thus the DNA nucleotide with adenine is called ATP, while the RNA nucleotide is called dATP

    The nucleotides composing the strands are connected via phosphodiester bonds. The strands are thus polarized with the 5' end being the one with the exposed phophate, while the 3' end being the one with a 3-hydroxyl group on the deoxyribose

    The nucleotides always exists in the same Watson-Crick Basepairs across the helix
  • Thymine Adenine (Two hydrogen bonds) (In RNA Thymine is swapped with Uracil)
  • Cytosine Guanine (Three hydrogen bonds)

    The helix is formed by the hydrogen bonds between the base pairs. The strands are antiparallel meaning that the 3' end of one strand is next to the 5' end of the other.
  • The distance between the strands is asymmetrical with the turning, meaning that one "turn" - (The major groove) is wider than the other (The minor groove)
  • Each turn is 10.4 bp, and there is 0.34 nm between nucleotides, giving nm pr turn

    The helix of the DNA can be denaturated (Locally split in two) and renatured again. This is an essential feature of the DNA.

    Normally this is enzymatically catalyzed, but heat can also "melt" the strand connections. This not an absolut or linear process, but convergent - thus we define the melting temperature as the point of 50% of the strand (hydrogen bonds) being disassociated.
  • is dependent on the number of hydrogen bonds which is determined by the ratio of A=T (Two bonds) to G=C (Three bonds) and the length of the DNA.

The Genome

The genome is all the DNA in a given celle (and organism).

  • The human haploid (Single chromosomes) genome consists of bp

    The diploid human genome is divided in 46 chromosome pairs (Including the two x or x+y)

Only a small proportion of the chromosomes (and genomes) are transcribed, and even less is translated to proteins.

  • About 1.5% codes for proteins in the humane genome
  • Not all genes code for protein, a gene is (loosely) defined as a segment of DNA that is transcribed.


Organization of the genome

There is 2 meters of DNA stuffed into in a nucleus of 10 . This is possible due to the structure and organization of the chromosomes.

The basal fiber of the chromosomes is chromatin. This is this beads-on-a-string like fiber, with DNA sequentially wrapped around histones.

The nucleasomes constists of DNA wrapped around large histones, as well as linker DNA linking the wrapped histones.

  • There are 200 base pairs per nucleosome (including the linker of bp)
  • This gives bp wrapped about the histone

The nuuclease, MNase, can cleave the linker DNA releasing nucleosome core particles

The nucleosomes can be supplemented by histone H1 (Linker Histone)

  • Unlike H2A/B, H3 and H4 it isn't part of the bead, but sits on top of it
  • It locks the linker DNA in place, though to be condensating the chromatin

The chromatine is formed by the nucleosomes, which in turn forms the chromatine fiber of packed nucleosomes.

  • This fiber is folded in loops to the mitotic chromosome
  • This is 10,000 times shorter than the DNA length

Cellular specialization as a result of chromosome structure

All of the genome is present in every cell of the body (sort of). Then how the different cells express different types of function

Epigenetics! - The nucleosome position and chromatine structure is different between cell types.

  • This can be controlled by transciption factors
  • Because some parts of the chromatine is compact - heterocromatine , it cant be transcribed in contrast to euchromatine which is active
  • This is how different cell types, can control which genes is transcribed - a lot if these being translated to proteins

The figure shows how the chromatin structure allows two cells to express similar genes (Red) and different genes (Green and Purple)

The chromatine can be remodeled by remodeling complex, which can make condensed chromatin - decondenced

  • Using ATP they modify the structure of nucleosomes and the overall structure of the chromatin

    The N-terminal tails of the histones can be modified in a way, that modifies the conformation of the histones. There are very many possibilities for modifications

  • If there is a lot of acetylisation, the histones are often decondensed

    • The positively charged lysines are attracted to negatively charged DNA, leading to condensed chromatin
    • When these are acetylated it leads to neutral charge, and thus less condensation

  • Phosphorylation can both works for condensation and decondensation of chromatin

    • Used commonly in DNA damage reparation

  • Methylation can also both work for condensation and decondensation

    • Lysine and arginine can be methylated
    • A single lysine can have mono- di and tri-methylation
      Depending on the modiffications they can code for histone codes

An example of the complexity is trimethyl on the H3 tail, which results in heterochromatin formation and gene silencing - but if next to acetylation, it will lead to gene expression

Protein code readers binds to the different histone codes / modifications, which lead to

  • Gene transcription
  • DNA synthesis
  • DNA repair

DNA methylation

This is a process where the activity of a DNA segment can be changed, without changing the sequence. The methylation usually occurs on cytosine, but also adenine at times. The methylation of cytosin usually leads to gene silencing

  • A part of the aging process
  • Involved in hippocampal long term memory
  • Cancer

Chromosome structure

The chromosomes has three regions of interest during the cell division

  • Telomeres is the end part of every chromosome - Formed of heterochromatin, this is slowly degraded with age (and enough cell division)
  • Replication origins is the place where the replication of the DNA begins during interphase
  • Centromers is the site of assembly of the kinetochores during mitosis (Chromosome segregation) - Formed of heterochromatin

DNA in Embryos

In a embryo cell, we see the epigenetic factors will inactivate one of the X chromsones (in biological females)

  • Th entirety of the one chromosome is "inactive", which is passed on through cell division
  • Thus only the genes of one of the X chromosomes are expressed
  • This is controlled by acetylsation of the histones

We not only inherit genome, but also the chromatin structure and thus histone modifications
