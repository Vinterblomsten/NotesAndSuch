###### **Cognitive Psychology**
A basic assumption of [[#**Cognitive Psychology**|Cognitive Psychology]] is that we do not perceive and act in the world. Rather, out perceptions, thoughts, and actions depend on internal transformations or computations of information obtained by our sense organs. 

Experiments in cognitive psychology test hypotheses about mental operations by adjusting the input and measuring the output or reaction. This helps us gain insight into how how the brain manipulates and processes information. This often requires at least one **independent variable** which is the input that we can control, and then at least one **dependent variable** which is the event we are evaluating such as response time or number of correct responses. We always look at the effect of changing the independent variable(s) on the dependent variable(s). Experiments always raises new questions which we can test by changing the independent or dependent variables, and elimination "noise" which might be observed and considered when testing. 

The dependent variable, that is the measurement, can be a list of things, but the most used variables are things such as reaction time. Correctness is another used metric, both as a quantitative measurement (number of correct responses), and as a qualitative meassurement (how is the answer answered correct or not). Often this in turn will be compared with response time.

The two key concepts that underlie the cognitive approach is:
1. Information processing depends on [[#Mental representations]]
2. These [[#Mental representations]] undergo [[#Internal Transformations]]

The fundamental goals of cognitive psychology include identifying the [[#Mental representations]]
that are required to perform cognitive tasks, and exploring the [[#Constraints on Information Processing|Limitations in task performance]].
###### Behavioralism
Behavioralism in cognitive psychology is an approach that emphasizes observable behaviors over internal mental processes, focusing on how environmental stimuli shape responses through conditioning and reinforcement. While traditional behaviorism, as pioneered by figures like John B. Watson and B.F. Skinner, largely dismissed mental states as irrelevant, cognitive psychology integrates behavioral principles with an understanding of internal cognitive processes such as memory, attention, and problem-solving.

##### Mental representations
All information processing rely on mental representation, which vary greatly depending of the context. For example a ball can be represented picturally, linguistically, or mathematically, all of which are very different representations. This means that different representations uses different mental operations, which can be seen in the letter-matching task, where we operate differently (perhaps hierarchically) by representing letters visually, by their identity (a and A is the same), or by category: ![[Screenshot 2025-02-01 at 09.32.13.png]]


##### Internal Transformations
Transformation of mental representation happens all the time, for example when we read a book and create a mental picture, or when we smell a meal that reminds us of our childhood. **Taking action** often requires translation of perceptual representation into action representation in order to reach a goal.
Take note though, that it is not a simple sequential process from sensation to perception to action, but it is altered by and depending on memory, as is theorized in [[#Behavioralism]].

###### Serial vs parallel processing
A lot of cognitive experiments and theories are centered around serial or sequential vs parallel processing. For example: Sternberg (1975) showed that memory comparison appears to be a serial process, meaning that the reaction time for comparison is dependent on the number of comparisons.![[Screenshot 2025-02-02 at 16.10.04.png]]
Sternberg postulated four primary mental operations:
1. Encoding. The participant must identify the visible target.
2. Comparing. The participant must compare the mental representation of the target with the representations of the items in memory.
3. Deciding. The participant must decide whether the target matches one of the memorized items.
4. Responding. The participant must respond appropriately for the decision made in Step 3.'

###### Constraints on Information Processing
Exploring the limitations in task performances is a central concern for cognitive psychologists. This is as an example the Stroop effect, which is seen in the Stroop task of recognizing colors of words, some of which are the name of another color:
![[Screenshot 2025-02-02 at 16.22.01.png|400]]
The Stroop effect powerfully demonstrates the multiplicity of mental representations. Interestingly we can reduce the interference from the words by requiering a key press rather than a vocal response. Thus the word-based representations are closely linked to the vocal system, but not necessarily other systems. 

###### Reactions times
When talking about reaction time we differ between **simple reaction time (SRT)** which is defined as perception and a following motor response, whereas **choice reaction time (CRT)** introduces a choice, so it is not a simple reaction of a visual cue, but the test subject has to reason before reacting.
