The **Retina** recieve light waves and transform them to neural signals it is projected to the **[[Peripheral Nervous System (PNS)#Thalamus|thalamus]]** (letral geniculate nucleas , LGN) via the **optic chiams** (a crossing) and the **optic tract**. It is then sent to the primary visual cortex. 

##### The Retina
The retina is 0.5mm thick, as made of desnely packed layers of neurons, the deepest being composed of millions of photoreceptors that contains **photopigments** (protein molecules that split apart when exposed to light.

 **The cellular function of Photorecepters**
![[Screenshot 2025-02-10 at 10.36.59.png]]

Activation by light hyperpolarizes the cell.
There is a constant influx of sodium when not activated, which is deactivated (the $Na^+$ channels close) causing a hyperpolarization into a for more negative electrical potential (opposite of a regular action potential). 

**Photorecepter adaptation** 
Photorecepters work over an increible range of lightintensities. The range is about 10 orders of magnitide.
The photorecepters code light intensity relative to the current level of surrounding brightness intensity.
The adaptation takes a while cause it is a biochemical process and not a bioelectrical one. 

**Two types of Photorecepters** at the back of the retina. 
The foveal vision has the greatest concentration of cones, and the greatest amount of convergence
The parafoveal (peripheral) has a far greater density of rods than cones

- Rods: Contain **rhodopsin** - Are more easily excited to light, specialized to night vision, high amplification (single photon detection), low temporal resolution, 
	- The ratio between rods and the ganglion cells acivated by them is 260 million to 2 million
- Cones: Contain **photopsin**- Cromatic (have three different subtypes of photoreagents and this allow colour vision), less excitable to light,
	- The ratio between Cones and the ganglion cells are much more equal with a few cones per ganglion cell - providing a much sharper image

**Convergence** meaning multiple presynaptic cells signal to fewer postsynaptic cells. High convergence is about 100:1 - In contrast to **Divergence** in which fewer presynaptic cells signal to multiple postsynaptic cells.

##### Optic Chiasm
 Before entering the thalamus, in the optic chiasm, the optic nerve splits into two parts: the temporal (lateral) branch traverse accros the ipsilateral side, and the nasal (medial) branch cross to the contralateral (opposite) side, thus the right visual field and the left visual field from each eye ends up in the same contralateral position in the thalamus and the visual cortex. 


![[Screenshot 2025-02-10 at 10.26.08.png|500]]

##### The Thalamus (LGN - Lateral geniculate nucleas)
The thalamus has a six-layer structure with morphological differences between the first four and the lower two 
- The top layers are made up of parve-cellular layers (Small cells) - Foveal vision (less convergence)
- The buttom layers of made of magno-cellular layers (Large cells) - The peripheral vision (More convergence)
The layers are separated by the eye the signal is coming from.

Not much going on in terms of input/output

### Receptive Fields and object recognition
"the stimulus region and features that affect the activity if a cell in a sensory system"

The nature of a receptive field of a cell can inform us about its function
- With the help of receptive fields we can decribe 

Cells early in the visual system (eg. retina) have very small receptive fields (react only to a stimuli in a very specific place in the visual field), whereas cells later on in the system have larger receptive fields (less spacial specificity)

 **Perception of Luminance**
![[Pasted image 20250210112123.png|400]]

Ganglion cells are divided into two types

**Type I: centre-on cells**: If light falls into the centre, but not the surrounding, the cell fires  frequently - If light falls into the surrounding but not the center, it does not fire - and **if it falls in both** it fires weakly (low frequency)

**Type II centre-off cells**:  Opposite of above

#### Colour Perception
Colour is a Perceptual quality, based on the variety in wave length of photons. 

Colour **Contrast** - stimuly with the same wave length and luminence can be percieved as different colours,
and  colour **Constancy**:  different wave lengths can be percieved as the same colour all based upon the context of the colour. 

The **Neural basis of colour perception** is again based on the three kinds of cones in the retina - **trichromatic** vision - leading the to RGB-setup of most colour representation (three dimensional) 

Interestingly we are very good at distinct red colours from green colour, even though they are very close in terms of wave length. 

**On/Off cells**
Like with the rods we have some on/off ganglion cells in colour vision, where they difer between different colours in the visual field 
![[Pasted image 20250210114905.png|400]]
The blue/yellow distinction uses a mixture of the other two cones, to detect the yellow colour.

##### Top-Down Effects
In addition to the bottom-up perspective of visual processing, the brain uses top-down processes as well in object recognition, meaning that it includes the context or the expected visual input already early in the recognition process. This allows us to not having to recognize the entire "scene" in our field of vision at every moment.
