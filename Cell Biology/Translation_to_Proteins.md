Translation is the process of translating mRNA to proteins
#### Ribosomes
Ribosomes are an important part of the translation. A **polysome** is a series of ribosomes that **simultaneously** translates the same mRNA molecule leading to many copies of the translations in a short interval of time. 
- This is why we see the **christmas tree**-like structures in a **miller spread**
<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-09-29%20at%2017.18.55.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-09-29 at 17.18.55" width="360">

The ribosome it self consist of a **large subunit** and a **small subunit**
- The large subunit consists of **3 rNA** molecules and **39 ribosomal proteins** (MW=2,800,000)
- The small subunit consists of **1 rNA** molecules and **33 ribosomal proteins** (MW=1,400,000)
<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-09-28%20at%2013.19.13.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-09-28 at 13.19.13" width="416">

There are **differences** between **eukaryotic** and **prokaryotic** ribosomes
- The **size** of the subunits differ in a way, that creates structural change, meaning that we can design **antibiotics**, that **targets only the units of the prokaryotic** ribosome.
- The location also differes, where the prokaryotic ribosomes are mostly in the cytosol, the eukaryotic are both in the cytosol and the ER

The ribosome has **three tRNA binding sites** called
- A - Aminoacyl site
- P - Peptidyl site
- E - Excit site

![Screenshot 2026-09-29 at 17.26.00](../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-09-29%20at%2017.26.00.png)

The ribsome is in itself a **ribozyme**, meaning that it is an RNA molecule with catalytic activity (opposed to enzymes, which must be translated from mRNA first) 
- The RNA in the large subunit **catalyzes peptide bonding** at the **P site** - (specifically the ribozyme **peptidyl transferase**)
- The catalytic activity is dependent on the **RNA folding correctly** (secondary and tertiary structure)
#### Ribosomal tRNA
The **tRNA molecules** match the amino acids to codons in the mRNA. The tRNA is trascribed by the **RNA polymerase III**, unlike the mRNA. 

There are 64 possible codons
- 61 amino acid coding codons (Including the start codon "AUG" - Methionine)
- 3 stop codons

The tRNA includes four double stranded segments by complementary base pairing
- The secondary structure forms a cloverleaf like shape, with **three loops**
- The tRNA folds into an **L-shape** (tertiary structure)

The nucleotides on the tRNA **matching the given codon** on the mRNA is called the **anticodon** on the **anticodon loop**.

The tRNA can not it self recognize and bond to the correct amino acids - This is the role of **aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase**, which catalyzes the **peptide bond** formation (between the **carboxyl group** of the amino acid and the **tRNA 3' end**) of the amino acid corresponding to the tRNA codon, using **ATP hydrolyzis** as energy.
- There is **a version** of aminoacyl-tRNA synthase for **each amino acid** - that means 20 versions
![Screenshot 2026-09-29 at 17.52.58](../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-09-29%20at%2017.52.58.png)
This redundancy of codons to amino acids is also a result of some of the tRNAs allowing a **wobble** (mismatch) in the third nucleotide - this is why a lot of the codons for the same amino acids only varies in the third nucleotide. 
#### Translation 
The translation of a complete polypeptide chain in an mRNA molecules has three steps
- Initiation
- Elongation
- Termination
##### Initiation
The translation always **starts at "AUG"** codon, followed by the **coding sequence**
	<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-09-29%20at%2017.56.58.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-09-29 at 17.56.58" width="280">
- In prokaryotes the AUG can occur multiple times on the mRNA making the prokaryotes **polycistronic**. This means, that prokaryotes don't rely of the AUG for starting, but has special **ribosome-binding sites**, upstream of each start codon. (In prokaryotes the 5' UTR is called the **LEADER** and the 3' UTR is called the **TRAILER**)
	- Here the ribsome binds to the ribosomes binding side (**RBS**) 5-10 nts upstream from the start codon with **consesus codon 5'-UAGA**
	<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-09-29%20at%2017.55.59.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-09-29 at 17.55.59" width="483">
Since the starting codon "AUG" also codes for the amino acid **methionine**, all polypeptide chains begins with this, but it is **usually cleaved off**.

A special **initiator tRNA (Met-tRNA)** is needed to start the translation at the "AUG" codon.
- This initiator Met-tRNA is different from the normal **elongation Met-tRNA**, which cannot initialize the translation
The initiator tRNA forms complex with the **small ribosomal subunit** and some **translation initiation factors** 

Before the initialization can commence, special **proteins at the 5' cap** and at the **poly(A) tail** is **required**

The complex binds to the mRNA at the **5' cap** and scan for the first start codon. The first AUG thus defines the **reading frame.** When found, the initiator tRNA binds to the AUG, the translation initiation factors dissociate, and the **large subunit** binds with the initiation tRNA in the **P site**.
- This leaves the **A site empty**, for the second charged tRNA (with an amino acid bound)

Eukaryotes make **monocistronic** mRNAs, where only one protein / molecule is decoded, while **prokaryotes** make **polycistronic** mRNAs where multiple proteins can be decoded per mRNA

##### Elongation
After the start codon has been recognized, the initiation factors dissociate, and the **large ribosomal subunit** binds to the complex. Elongation herefrom commence in four steps
1. A charged tRNA base-pairs with the codon in the **A site**
2. In the in the **P site**, the C-terminus of the polypeptide chain uncouples from the tRNA and instead forms a **peptide bond** with the **amino acid in the A site** 
3. The **large subunit shifts** such that the P site becomes the E site, and the A site becomes the P site. Thus the A site is once again empty
4. The **small subjunit** also shifts, **ejecting** the **tRNA** at the E site 

This sequence of action then repeats over and over.
<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-09-28%20at%2014.42.06.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-09-28 at 14.42.06" width="266"><img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-09-28%20at%2014.42.16.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-09-28 at 14.42.16" width="269">

The mRNA sequence is **decoded** into the codons along the translation process.
- Errors resulting in a missing or added nucleotide, can **shift the entire reading frame**, meaning that every resulting codon is different. 
- Since the codons come in nucleotide triplets, there is **three different reading frames** or mRNA
![Screenshot 2026-09-28 at 13.38.11](../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-09-28%20at%2013.38.11.png)
##### Termination
When a stop codon enters the A site, it is bound by **release factors** instead of **tRNA**. The release factors adds an $H_2O$ to the polypeptide chain, freeing the C-terminus of the chain. The ribosome then releases the mRNA and the different parts dissociate.

##### Post Translation
The final translated polypeptide is **not yet the finished protein**, as it must fold correctly, it can covalently bind to cofactors, it can by post-translationally modified, etc.

At the end of life for proteins, the **proteasomes** break back the proteins into amino acids
- Short-lasting proteins
- Misfolded / damaged proteins

It is signaled by **polyubiquitin chains** bound to the target protein. These Ubiquitinated proteins are unfolded and fed trough the **central cylinder** of the proteasomes, where the polypeptide chain is degraded into amino acids, and the ubiquitin is recycled.
