#### Transcription regulators (Protein-DNA interaction)
Proteins can act upon and regulate the DNA transcription directly. There are many of these **transcription regulators**, many of which are **transcription factors** (but not all of them). 

During transcription the **general transcription factors** (Or Sigma factor for prokaryotes) bind to the **(core) promoters**, binding RNA polymerase and initializing transcription. In the upstream region of the promoters, there are regulatory **promotor regions**, where other **transcription regulators** can bind to **activate** or **repress** the general transcription factors.
 
![Screenshot 2026-09-28 at 16.29.44](../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-09-28%20at%2016.29.44.png)

The transcription factors interact with the **major groove** of the DNA, as the chemical information is more accessible. Here some chemical groups in the nucleotide bases are exposed. This allows for
- Hydrogen bonds
- Hydrophobic interactions
- Electrostatic interactions

The different base pairs thus provide different binding possibilities - including the same base-pair flipped. This proves the **specificity** of the DNA-Transcription factor interaction:
![Screenshot 2026-09-28 at 16.33.08](../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-09-28%20at%2016.33.08.png)
The transcription factors usually interact with the DNA in the form of **$\alpha$-helices** forming some of the structures
- **Homeodomain**: Low specificity - Three $\alpha$-helices, the third binding into the major groove
- **Zinc fingers**: $Zn^{2+}$ ion stabizes small domains - usually in Cys-His sequences
- **Leucine-zipper**: Dimerizes the protein, in a way such that the adjacent areas can bind to the DNA

![Screenshot 2026-10-04 at 09.24.39](../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-04%20at%2009.24.39.png)Per  $\alpha$-helix we can only bond to 2 or 3 base pairs, thus multiple $\alpha$-helices are usually needed to interact with the DNA.

The transcription factors can bind in different places on the regulatory promoter region, having **different effects** on the transcription- all dependent on the genes and promotor regions them selves. 

#### Prokaryotic Transcription Regulation
Bacteria have a small genomes of merely 4 million bp. With only 4000 proteins, and 60% active at any time, they need to be very adapative to the environment in terms of gene expression. 

The genes in the bacteria are often arranged in **OPERONs**, in which **multiple genes** are expressed by the **same promotor** (and promotor region) - The result can be **simultaneous synthesis** of enzymes that act in the **same pathway**, called **coordinated expression**. This coordinated expression of the **OPERON** are controlled by a DNA segment called the **operator**. 
- The operator can be **activating** or **repressing** dependent on the transcription factor** bound to the operator. 

**Example**: The E.coli tryptophan operon (**Trp operon**) - a **binary switch**
- When **tryptophane** is high, the tryptophane binds to the repressor, which is **activated** and binds to the operator, **restricting the transcription**
- When it is low, the **repressor is inactivated**, and releases the operator, allowing the RNA polymerase to transcribe the gene. 
- Thus the trypthophan repressor is a **transcriptional repressor**
![Screenshot 2026-09-28 at 16.48.04](../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-09-28%20at%2016.48.04.png)

Some other prokaryote regulators **activates** instead of repressing the transcription. Here the **promoter** is only marginally able to bind and activate RNA polymerase. These ineffecient promoters become fully functional with the binding of the **transcriptional activator**.

An example of a gene with a **transcriptional activator** is the **lac operon** in e.coli, which codes for a **lactose utilitization** pathway. This operon has both an activator and a repressor
- The activator region binds to **activated CAP**. This is activated in the case of **absent glucose**, which increases cAMP production, that in turn activates CAP.
- The repressor region binds to the **Lac repressor**, which is activated in the **absense of lactose**, thus stopping the gene expression

The result is and OPERON that is activated only if 
1. Glucose is absent
2. Lactose is present
Which means that the ressources is used most efficiently (Ressource economy)

#### Eukaryotic Gene Regulation
The eukaryotic gene regulation is more complex than the prokaryotic regulation. 

**Many general transcription factors** are needed for recruiting RNA polymerase, not just sigma factor as in prokaryotes. 

However, Promoter regions can extend far upstream from the core promoter, also **indirectly interacting** with the RNA polymerase The **combination of these transcription factors** have direct transcriptional regulation.

Additional regulatory regions can be **further away** from the the promoter regions These are called the **enhancers** and the **silencers**. They bind to TFs called **activators** and **repressors** respectively. These are brought together with the promotor by **mediator protein complexes**
<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-09-28%20at%2016.56.46.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-09-28 at 16.56.46" width="522">

The **loops** in the DNA restrict activity of expressers and silencers, and they are formed by **COHESIN** (Chromosome loop-forming clamp proteins). The loops are also called **topological associated domains (TADs)**

<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-04%20at%2010.05.00.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-10-04 at 10.05.00" width="572">

In general the eukaryotic regulation of transcription as a coordinated process, with many parts working together
- Promoters - The local regions near to gene that allows TFs to affect the expression 
- Transcription factors - The proteins binding to the promoters
- Enhancers (And silencers) - The non-local **regions** that transcription regulators bind to affects the expression
- Mediator - The **protein complex**, that brings together the different copmonents of the trascritption regulation
- RNA Pol II - The actual machinery, that is activated / inactivated by the different regulators
- TADs - The loops formed by the segment of DNA that includes all parts of the transcription and regulation
### Epigenetics
While every cell in the body contains the same genome, we find that of the **20.000 protein-coding genes**, only 50-60% are expressed
- 8-9k are **housekeeping genes** shared between all cells
- 2-3k are **cell specific** expressed in specific types of cells
- The rest are **conditionally expressed**, silent normally, but expressed under specific conditions

The expression of a gene is very much dependent on the **epigenome**. 

<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-09-30%20at%2011.19.46.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-09-30 at 11.19.46" width="268">

By definition **epigenetics** refers to **heritable**, **reversable** changes in the gene functions that don't change the DNA sequence. 

The **epigenome** thus sits "on top" of the genome - a collection of the compounds and modifications to the chromatin that regulates gene expression without changing the underlying DNA sequence. This also means that while the genome is **static**, the epigenome is **dynamic**

The **epigenetic mechanisms** are multiple:
- **Histone modifications**
- **DNA methylation**
- Chromatin remodeling
- 3D genome organization
- Non-coding RNAs

##### Histone modification
The **histone modifications** is a way to change the **condensation** of the chromatine - that is whether the genes in the chromatine are expressed or not.

The **N-terminal** **tails** of the histones are the ones being modefied in three different ways:
- If there is a lot of **acetylisation**, the histones are often **decondensed**
	- The **positively charged lysines** are attracted to negatively charged DNA, leading to condensed chromatin
	- When these are **acetylated** it leads to neutral charge, and thus **less condensation**
- **Phosphorylation** can both works for condensation and decondensation of chromatin
	- Used commonly in DNA damage **reparation**, as a marker to recruit **repair factors**
- **Methylation** can also both work for condensation and decondensation
	- Lysine and arginine can be methylated 
	- A single lysine can have **mono- di and tri-methylation**
Depending on the modifications they can code for **histone codes**

##### DNA methylation
The **DNA methylation** is when the DNA methyltransferase (**DNMT**) adds a methyl group to the cytosine. **TET enzymes** does the opposite, removing the methyl group
- It only happens on the **CpG sequences** in the DNA. (5' - Cytosine - Guanine - 3')

The DNA methylation happens all over, but it is especially important on **promoters**, where methylation blocks the transcription factors, effectively **turning the gene off.**

Or alternatively by methyl-CpG-binding protein (**MBD proteins**) binding to the promoter, blocking the transcription factors

### Post-Transcriptional Modifications
There is multiple steps of Post-Transcriptional Modifications, but our focus is on
- RNA Processing control
- mRNA degradation control
- Translation control

After (during) the transcription we have the **pre-mRNA**, which needs **processing**
- Splicing
- Capping
- Polyadenylation

The mature mRNA also includes **untranslated regions (UTRs)** before the start codon, and after the stop codon. These play an important role in translocation, etc. [RNA Processing](RNA_Processing.html)
![Screenshot 2026-09-30 at 11.36.14](../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-09-30%20at%2011.36.14.png)

This gives os room for **regulatory intervention**. The UTRs include the 5' UTR before the start codon, and the 3' UTR after the stop codon.

In the **5' UTR** we **control the translation**
- When translation begins, the small ribosome subunit binds to the mRNA cap, and searches for the start codon. 
- **RNA-binding proteins** in the 5' UTR can block this search, and thus **inhibit the translation**
- In **prokaryotes** - Similar **stem loops (hairpins)** on the 5' UTR can **slow down** or **inhibit the translation** 

![Screenshot 2026-10-04 at 11.18.30](../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-04%20at%2011.18.30.png)

In the **3' UTR** we can regulate the **stability** of the mRNA. 
- The **poly A tail** gives stability to the mRNA, by protecting the end. Over time the tail degradates, so the longer the poly A tail, the longer the time of stability
- The RNA location sequences, the **zip code**, is also in the 3' UTR, making it able reach destinations such as the synapses in neurons
- **AU-rich elements (AREs)** are nucleotide sequences in the 3' UTR, containing many adenine and uracil, which can be bound by **stabilizing** or **destabilizing** proteins, thus **controlling the half life** of the mRNA
- **Alternative polyadenylation** - See below
- **MicroRNA mediated repression** - See below

##### Alternative polynadenylation
In the 3' UTRs, the RNA Pol II it signals for protein complexes to bind at the **polyadenylation signal** (AAUAAA), which cleaves the tail and **Poly-A Polymerase (Pap)** begins adding the Poly-A tail. 

More than 70% of genes, however  have multiple polyadenylation signals, which means that the cleavage can occur at different positions, which will affect the length of the 3' UTR, which in turn can effect the stability of the mRNA.
- **Longer** 3' UTR have more miRNA binding sites, more RBP binding sites and thus **more regulation**
- **Shorter** 3' UTR have fewer regulatory elements, making it undergo **more rapid translation** and in fact **more stability**

An **example** is the **Cyclin D1** in **cancerous cells**. 
- APA switches usage from **distal** to **proximal** polyadenylation signals
- This increase mRNA stability and protein expression
- The increase Cyclin D1 increases proliferation and oncogenesis (The formation of cancerous tissue)
##### Alternative slicing
The [alternative splicing](RNA_Processing.html), where exons are skipped, can be seen as a regulatory mechanism, as it can both form premature stop-codons inducing **nonsense mediated decay**, degrading the mRNA, and it can alter the UTRs, changing the regulation like in the case of alternative polyadenylation.

##### Non-coding RNAs
Some types of RNA are used for translation (tRNA and rRNA). Some however regulates other types of RNA:
- **Micro RNA (miRNA)**
- Small interfering RNAs (**siRNA**)
- Long Non-coding RNAs (**IncRNA**) -  Extracurriculum

##### miRNA
The most important regulatory RNA is the **miRNA**. We have about 2000 miRNA genes, and about **60%** of human protein-coding genes are regulated by miRNAs. These can be combined in many ways as:
- One miRNA can regulate hundreds of targets
- One mRNA contains mulitple miRNA binding sites

Usually the binding takes place in the **3' UTR**, but sometimes at CDS and rarely at 5' UTR. They primarily act as **fine-tuners** of gene expression, which means that they dampen the expression, instead of turning it off comepletly.

The **precursor miRNA (pri-miRNA)** is transcribed like other genes by the **RNA polymerase II**. It is then processed into short intermediates (**pre-miRNA**). This then forms the **RISC complex** (RNA-induced silencing complex) with RISC proteins. This goes searching for **complementary target** on mRNAs
- If it is an extensive match $\rightarrow$ mRNA is degraded rapidly by **nucleases** within RISC (We don't produce this our selves in the cells - used in research by siRNA)
- If it is a less extensive a match $\rightarrow$ Translation is reduced, mRNA **sequestered**, meaning that it is separated from the active pool of mRNA, and **eventually degraded** by nucleases in the cytosol

An example is the **miR-15a/miR-16-1** in B-cells, which inhibits the **BCL2** gene (anti apoptotic proteins), **resulting in apoptosis** 
- If the microRNA is removed (**leuchemia**), the BCL2 gene is not inhibited, and the **apoptosis is blocked**, which means that the cancer cells can grow and divide further

##### siRNA
These are used in plants and "lower" animals as a **defence mechanism**, and are also used in research to create gene **knock-down effects** (functional analysis), as well as is certain types of **drugs**.

During virus infection, cells are **infected by foreign double-stranded RNA**. Some of these are cleaved by DICER into double-stranded **siRNAs**. These form complexes with RISC proteins, which can **degrade** complementary RNA molecules
- For viruses they will degrade the other virus RNAs
- For knockdown procedures and as drugs, they will degrade specific mRNAs, which means that we can use siRNA so target specific genes, that can be "knocked out" of translation, while retaining the original DNA
