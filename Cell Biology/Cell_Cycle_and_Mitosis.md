Not all cells takes part of the cell cycle - some **differentiates**, becoming specialized cell types outside of the regular cell cycle, while others **proliferate** in the regulatory manner known as the cell cycle.

The cell cycle is defined as a regulated series of events, through witch the cell **grows**, the **DNA** **duplicates**, and the cell **divide** into two daughter cells. 

Proliferation is an important aspect of **embryonic development**, growth of **organs**, wound **healing** and tissue **homeostasis**. A lot of diseases however leads to/is a result of **uncontrolled proliferation** - fibrosis, autoimmune diseases and most importantly cancers.
- The 20 most common genes affected in cancer is cell cycle regulation related (Both oncogenes and tumor supressors)
### Phases of the Cell Cycle
The actual division of the cell into two daughter cells is know as the **M phase**. Between M phases we have the **interphase**, which is usually divided in three parts:
- $G_1$ phase - the cell **grows**
- S phase - (synthesis) the point of **DNA replication**
- $G_2$ phase - the cell **grows** and **prepares** for mitosis
<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-12%20at%2008.56.31.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-10-12 at 08.56.31" width="467">
A special **resting state** also exists, $G_0$ - this can be initiated in the $G_1$ given **unfavorable conditions** for proliferation, such as  **nutrient deprivation** with a lack of amino acids or glucose and **low oxygen** as well as **toxin presence**. It can also be a result of **low mitogenic signals** such as EGF and PDGF (Growth Factors) which normally activate cell cycle progression pathways.

This point in the cell cycle is know as a **restriction point** - where certain requirements must be met, for the cycle to continue into the S phase. This includes
- Sufficient **growth factors** that activates pathways that **promotes cyclin expression** (EGF, PDGF)
- Adequate nutrients and energy
- An intact genome controlled by **p53** and other sensors

### Cell Cycle Control
At multiple **checkpoints** in the cycle, will different systems control that everything is in order for continuing. 
- **Restriction point** between $G_1$ and S
- **$G_2$/M checkpoint** before mitosis, to assure that the DNA is correctly replicated
- Spindle Assemble Checkpoint (**SAC**) during mitosis
<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-12%20at%2009.40.54.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-10-12 at 09.40.54" width="535">
This **cell-cycle control system** governs the cell-cycle by cyclically activating and inactivating **key proteins** and complexes, that initiates the DNA replication, mitosis and cytokinesis. This is done by **phosphorylating** and **dephosphorylating** of these proteins by **cyclin-dependent protein kinases (CDKs)**.

The CDKs are **serine-threonin kinases**, and while they are always present, they are only active under certain conditions. For one, they are **molecular switches** dependent on the binding of their respective **cyclin**. This means that cyclin is the main regulator of CDKs. Thus different CDKs can becomes activated and inactivated at different points in the cycle. 

The **Cyclin-Cdk complexes** are named after their active point in the cycle, with some major being:
- $G_1$/S-Cdk initiating the S phase
- S-Cdk activating the replication throughout the S phase
- M-Cdk initiating mitosis

<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-12%20at%2009.55.51.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-10-12 at 09.55.51" width="552">

Each Cdk has a **specific Cyclin**, that is needed for it's activation. 

<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-12%20at%2009.52.06.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-10-12 at 09.52.06" width="529">

The **cyclin levels are cyclical** throughout the the cell cycle, rising and falling in oscillation. This is controlled through **transcription** and **proteolysis** (degradation)
<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-12%20at%2009.58.48.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-10-12 at 09.58.48" width="455">
The **abrupt** degradation of both **M-cyclin** and **S-cyclin** is facilitated by **anaphase-promoting complex (APC/C)** - a protein complex, that tags cyclins with a **chain of ubiquitin**, marking them for degradation by **proteasomes**. When the cyclin is degraded, the Cdk returns to its inactive form. 

The transcription of the cyclins are in turn regulated by **mitogens** (e.g. growth factors) activating pathways that initiates the transcription.
- One important such pathway involves the **retinoblastoma (Rb)** protein, which in it active state binds to and **inhibits some transcription regulator**.
- When the mitogen pathway leads to activated $G_1$-Cdk and $G_1$/S-Cdk, these will **phosphorylate the Rb**, inactivating it
- This leads to transcription of S phase genes including **S-cyclin**
<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-12%20at%2009.57.48.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-10-12 at 09.57.48" width="462">

Cyclin is not the only factor in regulation of the cell-cycle. Altough the cyclin concentration increases gradually through transcription, the activation of the Cyclin-Cdk complexes are abrupt. Thats because the **Cyclin-Cdk complexes** can contain **inhibitory phosphates**, and only upon removal of these can they perform their role. 
- **Inhibitory kinase (Wee1)** phosphorylates the M-Cyclin-Cdk complex, while **activating phosphatase (Cdc25)** dephosphorylates and thus activates them if the DNA is intact and ready for mitosis.
<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-12%20at%2010.26.42.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-10-12 at 10.26.42" width="545">

Another regulator is the binding of **Cdk inhibitor proteins**, which keeps the complexes inactive until the required conditions are met. 

Using these mechanisms to controll the cell-cycle, the **checkpoints** can controll entrance to the phases of the division:
- **Cdk inhibitors** blocks entry to the S phase (and cell cycle in general) until environment is favorable
- **The APC/C is inhibited**, when the chromosomes are not properly attached to spindle, delaying the exit from mitosis 
- While DNA replication in incomplete (or damaged) the **activating phosphatase - Cdc25** is inhibited, blocking entrance to the mitosis. When it is complete, the first M-Cdk phosphorylates inactive Cdc25 in a **positive feedback loop** providing the abruptness of the rise in activity of M-Cdk.

<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-12%20at%2010.32.20.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-10-12 at 10.32.20" width="310">

#### DNA Damage Checkpoints
DNA damage can **arrest** the cell cycle in all phases through several distinct mechanisms. The halting mechanism at the $G_1$ - S transition is well understood:
- **p53** is a transcription factor, that is normally inactive and quickly degraded by the proteasomes. 
- When DNA damage occurs, protein kinases **phosphorylates p53**, making it both stable and active
- This binds to the **p21 gene** regulatory region, enhancing transcription, leading to increased synthesis of the **p21 - a Cdk inhibitor**
<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-12%20at%2010.46.46.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-10-12 at 10.46.46" width="516">

#### S phase
The preperation to the DNA replication begins in the $G_1$ where the **origin recognition complex (ORC)** recognizes and binds the **replication origins**, at which point they recruit the **Cdc6 protein**. These recruits the **helicase**, which ultimately dissociates the strands. Once this **prereplicative complex** is in place, the replication is ready to proceed, but not initiated.

**S-Cdk** will when actiavted initiate the replication, by activating the helicase and recruiting the replication machine - [DNA Replication](DNA_Replication.html)

The S-Cdk laso inhibits re-replication, by **phosphorylating Cdc6 and ORC** thus inactivating them form forming another prereplicative complex.
<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-12%20at%2010.52.27.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-10-12 at 10.52.27" width="526">
If the DNA repliation stalls, the appearance of single-stranded DNA at the replication fork triggers the **inhibition of Cdc25** - preventing the removal of phosphates from M-Cdc, delaying the M phase until the replication is complete.

#### M-Cdk Regulation
A rise in the M-Cdk triggers the $G_2$-M transition. This is due to a number or processes:
- Degredation of nuclear envolope
- Condensation of chromatine
- Formation of spindle apparatus
- Fragmentation of the Golgi apparatus
- Chromosome segregation (via APC/C)

The **degradation of the nuclear encolope** is a result of M-Cdk **phosphorylating** **lamins** and **nuclear prores** in the nuclear envolope, which results in the lamin complexes dissociating. When they are dephosphrolylated, they can reform in a new nuclear envelope.

<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-12%20at%2011.32.30.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-10-12 at 11.32.30" width="600">

The **chromosome segregation** starts after the S-phase, where the **cohesin rings** keep the sister chromatids together. These cohesin complexes **are dissociated** by **active separase**, which it self is activated by the **APC/C**, degreading the **securin**, which is an **inhibitory protein** for separase. 

<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-12%20at%2011.34.54.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-10-12 at 11.34.54" width="401">

The APC/C is phosphorylated by M-Cdk, and ACP/C inhibits M-Cdk by makring the M-cyclin for degradation, so they are in an **negative feedback loop**.
#### Cytoskeletal components
There are two cytoskeletal components that play important roles in the mitosis. The mikrotubules forms the **mitotic spindle**, while actin and myosin filament forms the **contractile ring**.

##### Centrosomes
The **centrosomes** are the main spindle organization centers. They each consists of a a pair of **centrioles** surrounded by the pericentriolar matrix (amorphous material).

The **centrosome cycle** is the cycle of the centrosomes in parallel with the remaining cell cycle. 
The **S phase** hosts the **duplication of the centrosomes**. When the duplication occurs, both copies stay together as a single complex. 

When mitosis initiates, they separate, and each of them nucleates an array of microtubules called **aster**. These moves to each side of the cell, where they from the poles of the **mitotic spindle**.
<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-12%20at%2011.28.45.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-10-12 at 11.28.45" width="232">

The **motor proteins** helps in forming the spindle in the cell in the **prophase** and **prometaphase**. The interpolar microtubules  This means that when two microtubules spontaneously overlap, the **kinesins** stabilizes the structure, by forming **crosslinks** between them, also **decreasing** the chance of **depolymerization**.

The **dyneins** anchors the **astral microtubules** to the cell cortex and help **position the spindle** in the cell.

<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-12%20at%2011.51.06.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-10-12 at 11.51.06" width="309">

In the **metaphase**, the binding between chromosomes and microtubules are fascillitated by **kinetochores** and **connecting protein** complexes. The microtubules attach to the kinetochores at the centromeres of the chromosomes.  
- If the sequence of DNA at the centromeres have been altered, the kinetochore might not recognize it, and the chromosomes will fail to segregate properly.
The tension between the **sister kinetochores** given the **bi-orientation** of the chromosomes, signals the correct attachment at each side (SAC). Each kinetochore binds 20-40 microtubuli in mammals.

<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-12%20at%2011.52.45.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-10-12 at 11.52.45" width="458">

One of the three main checkpoints of the cell cycle is the **spindle assembly checkpoint (SAC)**. The SAC is important for correct spindle formation. It is a **complex of proteins** assembled at the kinetochoes, which prevents the seperation of chromatids until every chromosomes is properly attached to spindle **from both poles**. When this is true for all chromosomes, the SAC is turned off, and the **anaphase** begins.
- This is important for avoiding mis-segregation
- It inhibits the APC/C, thus **delaying the anaphase**

There are **three classes** of the microtubules during mitosis
- Astral microtubules are most in numbers
- Kinetochore microtubules bind to the kinetochores
- Interpolar microtubules are cross-linked by kinetin 
<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-12%20at%2012.08.18.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-10-12 at 12.08.18" width="398">

The Anaphase can be divided into and **A** and a **B** part. 
- In the A part, chromosomes are pulled **polewards**. This is fascilitated by **kinetochore micrutubule** shortening (Though to be loosing tubulin at both ends)
- In the B part, the motor proteins drive two forces of movement
	- The **kinesin** slides the overlapping **interpolar microtubules** away from each other - pushing the poles apart
	- The dynein is ancored to the plasma membrane, where it moves along the **astral microtubulis** - pulling the poles apart

![Screenshot 2026-10-12 at 12.08.48](../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-12%20at%2012.08.48.png)

The **cytokinesis** is driven by the **contractile ring** of the **actine and myosin**, which tightens until the plasma membranes meet, and the cell can be separated to two parts. First, the non-kinetochore mikrotubules recruits proteins where they meet, which signals **RhoA proteins** in the cell cortex, to assemble the contractile ring.

#### Defective Mitosis
Cancer cells are genetically unstable, which means that they divide in unusual ways. This creates cells with an enormous genetic variety, which means that a lot of different cells in the tumor have different properties, some resistant to different drugs. These chromosomal aberrations can include:
- Altered **gene dosage effect** tipping the balance towards proliferation
- Higher **stress tolerance**
- Metabolic flexibility
- The **evasion of checkpoints** in cell cycle
- Drug resistance

**Tetraplidy** something is when the whole genome is doubles, which can lead to cancerous cells.
- 30% of all tumors are triggered by problems in mitosis.
