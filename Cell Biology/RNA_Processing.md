- RNA polymerase I synthesizes pre-rRNA with **no cap**, **no polyadenylation** and some **self-splicing**
- RNA polymerase I synthesizes pre-rRNA with **7-methylguanosin cap**, **cleaving and addition of polyadenyl tail** and **ribozyme catalyzed intron removal (spliceosomes)**
- RNA polymerase III synthesizes pre-tRNA and 5S rRNA with **no cap**, **no polyadenylation** and some **enzyme-catalyzed intron removal**

The processing takes place in the **nucleus** for eukaryotes, while in the cytosol for prokaryotes (no nucleus). 

The processing from **nascent mRNA** to **mature mRNA** occurs **co-transcriptionally**, that is it begins while the the mRNA is being synthesized. This includes 
- **Capping factors**
- **Splicing factors**
- **Polyadenylation factors**

#### Processing the Ends
The 5' end and the 3' end of the nascent mRNA goes through multiple modifications. They both consist of noncoding sequences called the **UTRs**. 

The **5' end** goes through **RNA capping** already at the beginning of the transcription (after 25 nucleotides). This cap consists of a **7-methylguanosin** (Guanin nucleotide with methyl group) bound to the first nucleotide but **three phosphates**. 

The **3' end** goes through **polyadenylation** where the tail is first cleaved/**trimmed** by an enzyme at a particular sequence, where a second enzyme adds a series of repeating adenine nuclelotides. This **poly-A tail** is usually a couple hundreds nucleotides long.
- In some genes there are multiple possible **sites of polyadenylation**, which means that there can happen **alternative polyadenylation**, which can affect the stability of the mRNA.

They both play a functional role of **Transcript stability** and **Nuclear export** while the **cap** is involved in the **Initiation of translation** and the **poly-A tail** is involved in its efficiency.

### Processing Noncoding Regions
In eukaryotes, the protein-coding sequences are interrupted by non-coding intervening sequences called **introns**. The scattered expressed sequences called **exons** are usually shorter than introns and thus represent only a small part of the total length of the gene in DNA. Introns vary in length from a single nucleotide to more than 10,000. 
- Introns evolves much faster than exons
- Some eukaryotic genes have no introns (such as histone genes)

<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-02%20at%2014.36.57.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-10-02 at 14.36.57" width="650">

Note that the names intron and exon are used both about DNA and RNA

When the polymerase II transcribes a gene, this includes both exons and introns. However while the transcription continues the **RNA splicing** begins. In this process **introns are removed** and **exons fused** together. The splicing can finish both before and after the polyadenylation.

After RNA slicing, capping and polyadenylation, the mRNA is **mature** and **functional** for translation. Before those steps it is called **pre-mRNA** or precursor mRNA. 

While the intron system seems wasteful it offers the possibility of **alternative splicing** where exons can be skipped and thus combined in different ways. This allows multiple distinct proteins to be produced from the same gene.
- About 95% of human genes are thought to undergo alternative splicing

#### Spliceosome
Although there is no general structure of introns, there are a few **nucleotide motifs** (specific sequences), that code for the removal of the entire segment. 
- At the 5' end and 3' end of the introns, specific sequences code on **both** the intron and exon
- Somewhere in the middle and **adenine** forms the **branch point** of the **lariat**
<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-02%20at%2014.52.58.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-10-02 at 14.52.58" width="514">

This **lariat** is formed during the splicing process. First the 5' end of the intron is cleaved by a snRNP. The free end binds to the hydroxyl of the **adenine**, forming the ring called a lariat. This is then cleaved at the 3' end, while the 3' end of the preceeding exon and 5' end of the succeeding exons are fused.
<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-02%20at%2014.57.27.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-10-02 at 14.57.27" width="244">

The **RNA splicing** as facilitated by an **assembly** of **ribozymes** and **proteins** called the **spliceosome**. 
- The RNA in the **U1** and **U2 snRNP** is complementary to the **nucleotide motifs** in the ends of the introns, and they can thus recognize these segments.
- **U6 snRNP** swaps with **U1** and forms an **active site** with U2 catalyzing the cleaving of the 5' end of the intron, and the bonding with the adenine. 
- Lastly the **5' end is cleaved**, the **exon ends are fused** and an **exon junction complex** are left on the fused gap, have regulatory functions later on

#### Subcompartments of the nucleus
Given the enormous number of components needed for transcription and processing, the necessity for compartmentalization is massive. However, many of the components needed for transcription forms a loose molecular aggregate called **biomolecular condensates**. These condensates acts like RNA factories, and thus forms small **membrane-less compartments**. Similarly, can the components needed for DNA replications form biomolecular condensates, all of these subcompartments of the nucleus being kept together by **non-covalent forces**. 

The most prominent subcompartment is the **ribosome factory** called the **nucleolus** where rRNA is transcribed and processed, and the ribosomes are formed.

#### Transportation of mRNA
Because the transcription takes place in the nucleus, while the translation takes place in the cytosol, it is necessary to transport the mature mRNA out of the nucleus. However, all the left RNA (excised introns, broken RNAs) should be kept inside the nucleus. Thus the transport must be very **specific**.

It is facilitated by the **nuclear pore complexes**. When a mature mRNA is bound to the correct **proteins**, such as the **exon junction complex**, some **cap-binding** proteins and **poly-A** binding proteins. It is the **entire set of proteins** and not any single protein that allows the mRNA to be transported. 
- Note that correctly processed mRNA is not necessarily correctly transcribed. 

Any waste RNA in the nucleus is degraded and the nucleotides reused for future RNA.

#### Nonsense-mediated decay
When the ribosomes translate the mRNA, they displace all **exon junction complex (EJCs)** in their way. Thus if an mRNA is correctly transcribed, then all of the EJCs should be displaced when the ribosome reaches the **stop codon**, as no exon is entirely UTR. 

If however there has been an error in the transcription resulting in a frameshift, or another mutation, that results in a **premature stop codon**, it could be the case that the ribosome reach the stop codon, with EJCs still placed on the mRNA. In this case, the **release factors** interacts with the EJC, resulting in the **mRNA being degraded** by proteins in the nonsense-mediated decay (**NMD**) pathway.

#### mRNA degredation
An mRNA can be translated many times into proteins, and thus the longer an interval of time it excists in the cytosol, the more proteins can be produced. The **lifespan** of mRNA differs considerably depending on the **type of cell** and the **sequence**. In eukaryotes this can be less than 30 minutes up to more than 10 hours. It is often controlled by the sequences in the **3' UTR**. 

The mRNA is usually degraded after **decapping** or **deadenylation**. This is done by the **ribonucleases**. 

The full life cycleof mRNA is shown for but eukaryotes and prokaryotes. It is clear that the processing of eukaryotic mRNA is much more complex than that of the prokaryotes. This includes:
- 5' Capping
- Splicing
- Polyadenylation

This also exemplifies the eukaryotic need for a nucleus, to keep these processes separate.
