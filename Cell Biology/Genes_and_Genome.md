"DNA is more of a tinkerer than an inventor" - Reform over revolution

No gene or genome is entirely new, it is always a product of a former existing sequence potentially with small modifications. Small changes over a long period og time, however, leads to radical difference in total.

#### Germ-line and Soma
The **germ-line** is all of the cells of an organism, that eventually develop into germ cells (and gametes). All of the cells that doesn't develop into germ cells (specialized to some aspect), are called **soma**. Stem cells *can* develop to both germ-line cells or **somatic cells**. This **germ-soma differentiation** is a part of what differs **eukaryotic** cells from **prokaryotic**. As eukaryotic organisms multiplies via **sexual reproduction**, whereas prokaryotic cells use **asexual reproduction**.

Whether a **mutation** in the DNA of a cell is inherited by **offspring** is dependent on whether it takes place in a germ-line, in which case it is passed on to **every cell of the offspring**, or in a somatic cell, which depending on the cell can go completely unnoticed, but can also result in cancerous cells.

Because every genome change is directly inherited in prokaryotes, their evolution is much faster than for eukaryotic organisms.

### Molecular Mechanisms of Evolution
The small modifications resulting in a genome change are usually encompassed by the following mechanisms:
- Mutations with in gene
- Mutations in the regulatory DNA of a gene
- Duplication of genes with **divergence**
- Exon shuffling
- Transposition
- Horizontal Transfer

#### Mutations
Mutations to the DNA are usually a result of **errors in the replication**, that haven't been caught by the DNA repair systems.
- Although spontaneous mutations also occur for other reasons - see [DNA Mutations and Repair](DNA_Mutations_and_Repair.html)

They usually encompass
- **Changing** one or more nucleotides
- **Deleting** one or more nucleotides
- **Inserting** one or more nucleotides

As the mutation can happen at **any point of the genome** most of them have no effect on the gene trascription in the end. There are however two types, that affect the expression, and are though as a part of the genomic evolution:

##### Mutation within a Gene
This has direct consequences on the transcribed RNA, and proteins after possible translation. The effect of this varies greatly.

**Inconsequential** mutations er the **most frequent**. This is when the resulting change is **intronic** (In an intron), in a **wobble nucleotide** (same amino acid), changes an **unimportant** amino acid, or swaps an amino acid with **one with similar properties**. The result is neutral/silent, and it doesn't really affect the functionality of the gene.

**Harmful** mutations happens sometimes. These can result in **altered splicing**, loss of **functionality** or **stability**.

**Beneficial** mutations are rare (as evolutions has done a pretty good job). These results in **improved** activity or functionality, save **ressources** or improves interactions.

An **example** of a harmful mutation is the a **single nucleotide change** in the **$\beta$-globin gene**, where a **glutamate** is changed to a **valine**, resulting in a **non-polar side chain** on the surface of the hemoglobin. 
- The hemoglobin joins together at these non-polar side chains, resulting in a fiber structure, deforms the red-blood cells to "sickle shapes"
- This is called Sickles cell disease

##### Mutations in a Regulatory Region
These mutations doesn't directly affect the expressed gene, but they have can affect
- **When** the gene is expressed - during different stages of development or cell cycles.
- **Where** the gene is expressed - in which tissues and organs for example
- The **level** of transcription - for example lowering the amount of transcribed mRNA and thus proteins

An **example** is the beneficial mutation of **lactose tolerance**. As **infants** we express the gene for **lactase** allowing our cells to hydrolyse lactose into two glucoses.
- The promoter used to be activated only in infants
- Mutations to the promoter activated the expression in adults, allowing adults to enjoy the cold delicious drink that is milk from a cow (or a goat).

#### Gene duplication and divergence
A feature of **many of the genes** in the human genome (and many other species) is the they **exists in multiple variations** - resulting for example in very similar proteins that only differs slightly. 

This is a result of the **duplication** and **divergence** of genes. Genomic duplication can occur in different fashions during the replication or reparation. One typical way is through misallignment of homologous chromosomes during **meiosis** and thus **unequal crossing-over**, where large parts of the chromosome are duplicated - this is called **ectopic recombination**
<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-04%20at%2020.09.14.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-10-04 at 20.09.14" width="541">

Another mechanism is called **polyploidy**. This is duplication of the entire genome during meiosis, and though it is quite rare in animals, it is a part of our evolutionary history as it is suspected to have happened in **two rounds** in the vertebrate lineage, leading theoretically to **four copies of each original gene**. After the polyploidy follows a period of instability, where most duplications are lost, though a considerable fraction remains.

The existence of **two duplicate genes** means the the cell can "experiment" more freely with the second duplicate, without disrupting the function of the original gene. 
<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-04%20at%2020.27.56.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-10-04 at 20.27.56" width="566">

An **example** of this is the **globin** gene. A lot of insects and primitive fish only expresses a **single-chain globin**,that is only one globin gene. During our evolution, a duplication diffrentiated $\alpha$-globin from $\beta$-globin, and later duplication an diffrentiation resulted in four other differentiated versions, including **three fetal $\beta$-globin** version and **two adult** version. This have resulted in the four-chain hemoglobin, which consists of both $\alpha$ and $\beta$-globin, and which can **bind four oxygens cooperatively.**

#### Exon shuffling
The same **recombination** mechanisms leading to duplication of an entire gene, can also occur inside genes. If it happens inside an exon, it usually leads to loss of function, but if it happens in **introns**, the entire exons will be swapped (As introns are far larger than exons, this is quite more common).

Exons generally code for specific **functional domains** of the proteins, maybe as a result of the evolutionary exon swapping. This also means that the **exon swapping** results in swapping of **entire functional domains** of the proteins. 
- It is theorized that all of the 20,000 human genes are a result of the **duplication** and **swapping** of a few thousand exons

#### Horizontal transfer
Rare among eukaryotes due to the existence of sexual reproduction, it is quite common for bacteria to **transfer genetic code between species**. This transfer between different genomes is called **horizontal transfer**, and it is for example done by **conjugation** in bacteria. 
- Here a bacterial plasmid is replicated, exchanged and inserted into the other bacterial DNA.

#### Transposition
When looking at the human genome - we see that 50% is these "repetitive elements". Much of this *dark genome* is in fact what we call the **mobile genetic elements**. These are **parasitic** DNA sequences, that can move or multiply in the genome. This includes two types called **DNA-only transposon** and **retrotransposon**, that utilizes RNA as an intermediary state.

![Screenshot 2026-10-05 at 14.29.31](../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-05%20at%2014.29.31.png)

The **DNA-only transposon** is the only mechanism of transposition in **bacteria**, but they play only a minor role in the human genome. Some of these **jumping genes** can be moved to another location in the genome, so called **cut-and-paste transposition**, while other are duplicated to a now location, while retaining their existing place - **replicative transposition**

Typically each transposon encodes a specialized **transposase**, which mediates it's movement. Many also hosts **additional genes** - this is for example how many bacteria hosts genes responsible for their **antibiotics resistance.** 

![Screenshot 2026-10-05 at 14.35.33](../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-05%20at%2014.35.33.png)
The **retrotransposons** make of for most of the human transposons, and are unique to **eukaryotes**. These mobile genetic elements are first **transcribed** to RNA. These segment are then converted to DNA by first **reverse transcriptase** and later DNA polymerase. This new DNA segment is then **inserted** into the target DNA. 
<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-05%20at%2015.00.45.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-10-05 at 15.00.45" width="327">
Specific **retrotransposons** make up for very much of our genome, with **L1 (Line-1) elements** making up for 15% and **Alu elements** making up for 10%. The L1 encode it's own reverse transcriptase, while Alu elements rely on enzymes present in the cell. 

Transposons can cause **genetic changes** in multiple ways. Firstly the insertion of the transposon it self:
- To the **promotor/regulatory regions** can cause changes in regulation, activating/inhibiting the gene
- To the **exons** causes direct change to the gene and the resulting amino acid structure, worst case shifting the reading frame.
- Into the **splicing sequences** can cause disruptions to splicing, including an intron or excluding an exon. 

Examples include
- **L1** insertion into the **Factor VIII** gene - essential for blood clotting, resulting in hemophilia
- **Alu** insertion in the **TBXT** gene - causing a splicing change resulting in the lack of a tail in hominoids (abes and humans).

We also see **transposon mediated exon shuffling**. This is often a result of two transposons in close vicinity with **one or more exons in between**, where excision is done improperly, instead carrying the the exon to another gene, thus aiding in faster evolution.

### Comparative Genomics
The field of comparative genomics studies the evolutionary changes among species on the DNA level. It is about identifying **conserved** and **divergent** **features** in the genome. This helps us understand **gene function**, evolutionary relationships, and the **origins of biological traits**.

There are across evolutionary divergence some **homologous genes**, that are highly similar in nucleotide sequence as well as in biological function. These are encompassed by the **50% of human genes** the have been present in common ancestors 600 million years ago.

When comparing chimps and humans we find essentially **all genes shared**, with about 1.2% divergence in full genome sequence. We can "abduce" the genes of the common ancestor of humans and chimpanzees, by comparing the genetic sequences of our genes, and consulting another close relative when in conflict
<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-05%20at%2015.55.19.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-10-05 at 15.55.19" width="630">
We see a difference in the **number of chromosomes** in humans and chimpanzees (and other abes). This is likely a result of the human merging of the second and 24. chromosome, leaving humans with only 23.

When looking at the **ordering of genes** in the chromosomes, we see a surprising high similarity, with **90% of genes** that can be partitioned into blocks of **conserved synteny** between mice and humans, meaning that the they have retained their local "neighborhood". 

Within these regions, we can align the DNA an compare the nucleotide sequence in detail. Such analyses reveal that, even though **50% of nucleotides have diverged** between mice and humans, there exists some very similar regions very genetic change is not tolerated. Such regions are conserved by **purifying selection**, that is - the regions are so important for functioning, that changes have lead to extinction. 

By stacking up multiple species in these comparisons, we can identify **4.5% of human genome**, which consists of sequences that are **highly conserved** among other mammals - of which only **1/4 code for proteins**, while the rest are regulatory DNA or code for non-translated RNA such as rRNA. 

We also see the then **coding regions** in general are much more similar. When looking at introns and exons, we see **far more changes in introns**, as most of them have **silent effects**, while most exons changes are harmful. 

![Screenshot 2026-10-05 at 16.16.37](../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-05%20at%2016.16.37.png)

We can also use comparative genomics to identify duplication regions. When comparing the human and mouse **$\beta$-globin clusters**, we can infer that Alu and L1 element insertion in the beta globin region occurred after lineage separation of rodents and primates (But before the seperation of humans and chimpanzees, as they retain the same genes)
![Screenshot 2026-10-05 at 16.20.18](../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-05%20at%2016.20.18.png)

We can even identify a **few hundred fundamental genes**, that have been somewhat preserved for 3.5 billion years, such as some of the rRNA genes.


### The Human Genome
Some stats about the human genome
- 3 billion base pairs
- > 19,000 genes
- ~5.000 coding genes
- ~11.000 pseudogenes (non-expressed duplications)
- 1.5% is protein coding sequences
- 50% is repetitive sequences (including mobile genetic elements, simple repeats and duplications)

We have that no two human genomes are the same, and generally we differ with 0.1% between humans with about **70 new mutation pr. birth**. While most of the mutations are silent, few give rise to changes in appearance while others **make os sick**.
