**Signal transduction** is defined as the process in which one type of signal is converted to another.

There are four basic forms of communication between our cells
- Endocrine (hormonal) communication
- Paracrine (And autocrine)
- Neuronal
- Contact-Dependent
<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-02%20at%2008.25.02.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-10-02 at 08.25.02" width="531">
**Endocrine signalling**
The public, long distance signalling, in which special **endocrine cells** release signalling molecules into global distribution mechanisms (blood vessels in the human body), which affects many cells. The signalling molecules are called **hormones**.

**Paracrine signalling**
Short distance (local) communication using multiple **local mediator** molecules - an important one being **growth factors (GFs)**. These are released in the local **extracellular fluid**. This also includes **autocrine signalling**, which means that the cell it self is affected by its own signalling molecules.

**Neuronal**
Neurotransmitters and Recptors

**Contact-Dependent**
Communication between cells, where **membrane bound signal molecules** are recognizes by **membrane bound receptors**. This  requires that the cells make physical contact. The membrane bound signal molecules are usually called **delta**.

##### Binding points
The **contact point** of a signalling molecules is often dependent on its polarity.
- **Hydrophile** molecules usually bind on **cell-surface recepters**
- **(Small) Hydrophobic** molecules usually bind to **intra-cellular recepters**

Some of the hormones being able to diffuse across the membrane or being translocated across it:

These are all **transcription regulators**. They bind to the **corresponding receptors** in the cytosol
- The formed **receptor-hormone complex** is activated and translocated into the nucelus
- Here to bonds to **regulatory regions** of target genes, taking the role af **specific transcription factors**, either enhancing of silencing the gene

#### Membrane-bound Receptors
There are three groups of receptors on the membrane
- Ion channel-coupled receptors
- G-protein coupled receptors
- Enzyme-coupled receptors

The same ligand can activate multiple different receptors. This means that different types of **cells react differently to the same signalling molecules**. An **example** of this is **acetylcholine**:
- It decrease heratrate in **pacemaker cells** via G-protein coupled receptor
- It activates secretion in **salivary gland cells** via G-protein coupled receptor
- It initiate contraction in **skeletal muscle cells** via Ion-channel coupled recetor

<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-07%20at%2010.01.07.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-10-07 at 10.01.07" width="474">

The **length of time** between signalling and response vary greatly. 
- Acetylcholine stimulation of skeletal muscle cells activate ion channels, resulting in contraction in **miliseconds**
- Growth Factors can activate processes of growth and division take takes **hours**

There are **two principles** of receptor activated signalling from the membrane, for different types of signalling
1. The **fast** signalling through altered protein function leads to changes in the environment in **milliseconds to minutes**
2. The **slow** signalling through **transcription regulation** leads to altered protein synthesis and changes (Cell growth, divisions, apoptosis) in **minutes to hours**.

In general we can categorize the molecules in the signalling pathways into the
- Extracellular signal molecule
- Receptor protein
- **Intracellular signalling molecules** - They can 
	- **relay** signals 
	- **amplify** them such that a few signal molecules result in a large response
	- **integrate** multiple signals for a nuanced response
	- **distribute** signals to multiple effector proteins
	- modulate the response through **feedback** to previous pathway molecules
- **Effector proteins** - They can be
	- Metabolic **enzymes**
	- **Cytoskeletal** proteins - altering the shape or movement
	- **Transcription regulators**

For many of the receptors, the **extracellular signalling molecules** are called **first messengers**, which initiates a pathway that transduce/amplify **second messenger** (small intracellular molecules) - the message is passed **downstream**


Signalling sometimes functions as a **molecular switch**. This means that the signal can turn on/off a given protein. These molecular switches fall usually in the two categories
- **Protein kinase** which as activated by the signalling molecule **phosphorylating the protein**
- **GTP-binding proteins** which as activated by **GTP binding** 
![Screenshot 2026-10-02 at 08.38.43](../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-02%20at%2008.38.43.png)

Many of the proteins activated by phosphorylation are themselves protein kinases, which is called a **phosphorylation cascade** (E.g. MAP Kinase Kinase Kinase)
- The two main types of Protein kinases in the intracellular signalling pathways are **serine/threonine kinases** and **tyrosine kinases**. 

The **GTP-binding proteins** are activated by another protein swapping GDP to GTP, while they them selves are **GTPases** meaning that they can hydrolyse the GTP, inactivating them selves.
- They can be divided into **trimeric** GTP-binding proteins (G Proteins) and **monomeric GTPAses** 
#### Contact-Dependent
When the **delta** signal protein makes contact with the delta receptor (**notch**), the notch tail is cleaved intracellularly, which can act as a **transcription regulator** - influencing growth and cell division.

<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-07%20at%2010.27.58.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-10-07 at 10.27.58" width="341">

This is used for example in cell differentiation, where an early **neuron** expresses delta proteins in the plasma membrane, which activated nearby **notches** inhibiting them to differentiate to neuron them selves.
