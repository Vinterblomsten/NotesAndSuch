The first step in the **gene expression** is the transcription of DNA to RNA or **ribonucleic acid**.

#### RNA Composition
Like DNA, RNA is a linear polymer of of nucleotide subunits. It differs in two ways
1. The nucleotides are called **ribonucelotides** because they contain **ribose** as their sugar instead of deoxyribose
2. RNA contains the base **uracil** instead of thymine, which is chemically similar, and it forms the same **double hydrogen bond** to adenine
<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-09-27%20at%2013.04.36.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-09-27 at 13.04.36" width="313">

Structurally they differ quite a lot, as RNA doesn't form double strands, but stays in **single strands**, which means that it like can fold in a myriad of ways, just like polypeptide chains. This allows **RNA to carry out functions it self**, and not just function as an information carrier. 

<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-09-27%20at%2013.06.45.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-09-27 at 13.06.45" width="639">

#### Types of RNA
There are multiple types of RNA with different functions

**Messenger RNA (mRNA)** is the RNA that the **majority of genes** code for. mRNA usually carries information from **one gene**, to be **translated** to a **single protein**. (In bacteria one mRNA can code for multiple proteins). 
- **1-2%** of the transcribed RNA

**Noncoding RNA** is all the RNA that aren't translated to proteins. This is subdivided into multiple types it self:
- **Ribosomal RNA** or **rRNA** forms the structural core of the **ribosomes** and are thus very important is this allows the translation of mRNA. 
	- **80%** of the transcribed RNA
- **Transfer RNA** or **tRNA** selects specific amino acids and holds them in the place of the ribosome where they are to be incorporated into the protein
	- **10%** of the transcribed RNA
- **MicroRNA** or **miRNA** is a part of the regulation of gene expression
- **Small nuclear RNA** og **snRNA** is a part of the processing of **pre-mRNA**. 
- Many other types of noncoding RNA

#### RNA Polymerase
The transcription is quite similar to DNA replication. Roughly described the processes is:
- The DNA strands are disassociated in a given place - This gives us the **template strand** and the **coding strands (RNA-lignende streng)**
- Here the **RNA polymerase** begins sequentially adding **ribonucleotides** to a strand complementary the the nucleotides on one of the DNA strands, by mending the **phosphodiester bonds** of the RNA backbone. 
- The energy for the reaction is given by the **ribonucleotides** (ATP, CTP, UTP and GTP)
- The RNA chain is elongated in the 5' $\rightarrow$ 3' direction, just like in replication
- After being appended to the chain, the nucleotide and complementary ribonucleotide disassociate, and the **DNA strands reassociates**, while the RNA strand remains one-stranded. The product is called the **RNA transcript**.
<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-09-27%20at%2013.10.12.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-09-27 at 13.10.12" width="516">

RNA polymerase is different and somewhat simpler than DNA polymerase. 
- It **doesn't need a primer** to begin the transcription process
- There is **no proofreading** mechanism to correct errors, because the RNA is not permanent information storage, so it doesn't have as big consequences.
	- This means that an error occurs for every $10^4$ nucleotide

Because the RNA disassociated so rapidly, and the DNA helix reforms, there can be **multiple transcriptions** of the same gene overlapping. 
- For a medium-sized gene of 1500 bp, there can be dozens of RNA polymerase transcribing at the same time, allowing more than 100 RNA transcripts to be produced in an hour
- Often however, it is much less

In **eukaryotic** cells there is not one but three types of RNA polymerase, each responsible for different types of RNA
- **RNA Polymerase I** is responsible for most **ribosomal RNA**
- **RNA Polymerase II** is responsible for the **mRNA**, miRNA and some other noncoding RNAs
- **RNA Polymerase III** is responsible for **tRNA**, some rRNA and other RNAs
The focus throughout is on **RNA Polymerase II**

#### Promoters and Terminators
The transcription always begin at a **promoter**. The promoter is a gene region with specific sequences, that lies **upstream** of the starting point (The promotor is **not transcribed**). Because of the asymmetry of the promoter, it will only be recognized in one direction, which will start **transcription in the 5' $\rightarrow$ 3' direction** (reading the complementary 3' $\rightarrow$ 5' strand). The **terminator** is a similar gene region with specific sequences, that terminates the transcription at the **stop site** just after the terminator (and thus the terminator is transcribed). 

In **prokaryotic** cells the promoter is recognized by the **sigma unit** of the polymerase, which is released upon the beginning of transcription, and then rebound after the RNA polymerase is released from the DNA, activating it for a new transcription. Here the promoter is given by **consensus sequences** in the -35 and -10 spot (relative the the start of transcription).
![Screenshot 2026-09-28 at 09.29.04](../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-09-28%20at%2009.29.04.png)

In **eukaryotic** cells the sequences of the promoter is recognized by different **general transcription factors**. We have the following regions recognized by **consensus sequences**:
- **TFIIB** recognizes a sequence at -35
- **TBP** recognizes a sequence at -30 
	- This consists of mostly T and A nucleotides - know as the **TATA box** 
- **TFIID** recognizes a sequence at 0 (transcription start site)
- **TFIID** recognizes a sequence at +30
<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-09-28%20at%2009.45.39.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-09-28 at 09.45.39" width="527">

Because of the **asymmetry** of the promoter it is **directional**. This means that it can be present in both directions of the DNA, and gene transcription can take place on the 3' $\rightarrow$ 5' strand in both directions.
<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-09-28%20at%2009.40.20.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-09-28 at 09.40.20" width="515">

#### Initiation
In order to initiate the eukaryotic transcription, we require a set of **general transcription factors** to form the initiation complex. 
- Firstly the **TBP** subunit of the **TFII-D** binds to the **TATA box**
- This cause a **distortion** in the DNA double helix, forming a **kink** in the helix
- **TFII-B** binds to create a bridge to **RNA Pol II** which binds together with **TFII-E** and **-F**
- Lastly **TFII-H**  joins with two subareas serving different functions
	- The **helicase** part dissociates the strands at the transrciption start site. 
	- The **kinase** part which **phosphorylates the tail** of the RNA pol II
- Together they form the **transcription initiation complex** (The chronological order differs)

In order for the transcription to commence, it must be released from the complex. This happens with the **phosphyrylation of the pol II tail**, which releases most of the general transcription factors, which is then available for reuse.

#### Elongation and Termination
After dissociating the general transcription factors, multiple **elongation factors** joins the RNA polymerase II. These factors help the pol II move along the DNA, and they allow pol II to **acces the DNA in the nucleosomes**, by unwrapping it from the histones. 
<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-09-30%20at%2015.20.20.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-09-30 at 15.20.20" width="339">

Interestingly the mRNA slicing and further processing to **mature mRNA** occurs **co-transcriptionally**, that is it begins while the **nascent mRNA** is being synthesized.  
<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-09-30%20at%2015.33.50.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-09-30 at 15.33.50" width="324">

Once the pol II reaches the **polyadenylation signal** it is released from the mRNA. One released, the tail must be hydrolyzed before it can be used again.
