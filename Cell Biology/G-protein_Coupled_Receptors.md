The G-protein coupled receptors (**GPCRs**) are one of the two important receptor types in cell signalling, the other being [Enzyme-Coupled Receptors](Enzyme-Coupled_Receptors.md). They are also called **7TM receptors**, because they are **7-pass transmembran proteins**. 

The G-proteins 

#### General function of GPCRs
At "rest" the GPCRs are **inactive** and not bound to the **inactive trimer G-proteins**. When activated by a **signal molecule**, it will bind to the **$\alpha$ subunit** of the G-protein, also activating it. 

<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-07%20at%2011.04.23.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-10-07 at 11.04.23" width="402">

When **inactive** the G-proteins has a **GDP** bound to the $\alpha$ subunit. Upon activation, this is released, and it instead binds to a **GTP**. Thus the $\alpha$ subunit is a sort of **molecular switch**.

<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-05%20at%2008.25.25.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-10-05 at 08.25.25" width="357">

Except for the GTPase activity, the G-proteins doesn't perform other enzymatic activity, but instead **activates enzymes downstream** in the pathways.

The GPCR can in fact activate **many G-proteins in a single activation**, since they rapidly
1. Binds to the GPCR
2. Releases GDP and binds GTP
3. Dissociates from the GPCR allowing a new G-protein to bind

Some proteins **GEF** and **GAB** can help with the swapping of GDP to GTP, but they aren't essential.

#### G-protein families
We don't need to know every type of G-protein, but only the four important: $G_s$, $G_i$, $G_t$ (Transducin) and $G_q$

There are several types of G protein, that all share a common structure. They are composed of three protein subunits (trimer) called $\alpha$, $\beta$ and $\gamma$, two of which ($\alpha$ an $\gamma$) are **tethered to the membrane** via a lipid tail. 
- In some cases the **$\alpha$ subunit dissociates from the $\beta$ and $\gamma$** subunits upon activation, both complexes affecting target proteins.
- When the intrinsic **GTPase property** of $\alpha$ cleaves the GTP to GDP, the subunits return to their original associated state - this thus **controls the time interval** of effect. 

Many different GPCRs is activated through the **autonomous nervous system**, both the sympatic and parasympatic, but they are used throughout the entire body, and **all types of cell signalling**. 
#### $G_s$ and $G_i$ pathways
These two pathways regulate the **cAMP synthesis**, which then acts as **second messengers**, initiating effects in the cell.

When looking at the $G_s$ protein, we see that the $\alpha$ subunit activates the **adenyl cyclase**,  **catalyzing** the synthesis of **cyclic AMP**, as adenyl cyclase is an enzyme. 
- Later on the **phospherdiesterase (PDE)** will hydrolyse **cAMP** to **AMP**, both reactions being energetically favorable
<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-07%20at%2011.18.51.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-10-07 at 11.18.51" width="229">

The cAMP in turn activates the **Protein Kinase A (PKA)**, which has (at least) three **phosphorylation target proteins** - the specific response however depend on the cell type. 
- In skeletal muscles, **adrenaline** activates the $G_s$ resulting in the active PKA phosphorylising phosphorylase kinase, again phosphorylising **glycogen phosphorylase**, resulting in a breakdown of glycogen **releasing glucose**
- In heart cells, it increases heart rate and force of contraction
- In fat cells, it leads to breakdown of fat
- In adrenal glands, it leads to secretion of cortisol
<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-05%20at%2008.37.28.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-10-05 at 08.37.28" width="427">
Active PKA also phosphorylates some **transcription regulators**, activating them to regulate transcription of target genes.

$G_i$ is quite simply the opposite effect, because it inhibits the **adenylyl cyclase**, and thus the production of **cAMP**.

#### $G_q$ pathway
The $G_q$ proteins have a function (among others) of **raising the calcium levels** in the cells. 
It does so by the $\beta$-$\gamma$ subunits activating **phospholipase C (PLC)**. PLC **cleaves** the membrane bound **inositol phospholipid** into the two parts: **Diacylglycerol (DAG)** and **$IP_3$**.

$IP_3$ is released into the cytosol, where it binds to $Ca^{2+}$-channels in the ER ($IP_3R$), **releasing calcium intro the cytosol**.

DAG is a lipid bound to the **membrane**, where **it recruits** the protein kinase C (**PKC**), which is **activated by the released $Ca^{2+}$.** 

The PKC then phosphorylates a set of intracellular proteins **propagating the signal**.

<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-05%20at%2008.47.17.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-10-05 at 08.47.17" width="629">

Calcium is an effective signalling ion, because of the **very low concentration** of calcium in the **cytosol**, as a result of the **active transport** to the ER Lumen and extracellular space fascilitated by
- SERCA - an ATPase in the ER membrane, that transports $Ca^{2+}$ to the lumen
- PMCA - an ATPase in the plasma membrane, that transports $Ca^{2+}$ out of the cell
- $Na^+/Ca^{2+}$ antiporter

An important protein regarding calcium is **calmodulin**, which is activated by binding to $Ca^{2+}$. The Calmodulin-$Ca^{2+}$ complex has a wide range of target proteins, an important class of which is calmodulin-dependent protein kinases or **CaM-kinases**.
- When the **CaMKII** is activated by calmodulin, the kinase activity makes the protein autophosphorylate, such that it **stays active after $Ca^{2+}$ decreases**.  
- This for example plays an important role in synaptic plasticity during **LTP**

##### The physiological effect of Nitrogen Oxide on the endothel cells
Some **secondary messenger** are molecules that are small and hydrophobic enough to diffuse across the plasma membrane, and intro neighboring cells. 

An example of this is nitrogen oxide (**NO**) in the $G_q$ pathway in **endothelial cells**. In a **blood vessel**, the endothelial cells line the lumen, which is surrounded by **smooth muscle cells**, that **controls the pressure** in the vessel by **contraction**. 

<img src="../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-07%20at%2012.02.49.png" alt="Screenshot 2026-10-07 at 12.02.49" width="285">
When **acetylcholine** activates the $G_q$ protein leading to a release of $Ca^{2+}$. This $Ca^{2+}$ forms a **complex with calmodulin** to activate the **NO synthase (NOS)**, synthesizing NO.  
- The synthesis happens by **oxidizing arginine** with $O_2$ swapping a **nitrogen**, resulting in citrulline and NO
The NO diffuses into the nearby smooth muscle cells, where they bind to and activate the **guanylyn cyclase**, beginning the synthesis of **cGMP** leading to **relaxation of the muscles** and thus **decrease in pressure i**n the blood vessel - also what is called **vasodilation**
![Screenshot 2026-10-07 at 12.02.38](../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-07%20at%2012.02.38.png)

The cGMP is hydrolyzes to GMP after a while by **phosphodiesterase (PDE5)**. Thus by inhibiting PDE5 in the smooth muscle cells, we can  **retain cGMP** for longer periods, increasing the relaxation and blood pressure decrease. 

NO also plays a role in nerve cells. An **example** is the nerve ends in the penis, releasing NO to signal blood-vessel dilation resulting in erections. By **inhibiting PDE5** in these cells, we thus stimulate erections - the function of **sildenafil** known commercially as **viagra**

#### $G_t$ - Transducin
Transducins are important for the **photoreceptors** in the retina - a special sensory cell. There is approximately $10^9$ **rhodopsines** in the photorecepters, which are GPCRs. Via a vitamin A derivate molecule, that changes conformation with **stimulus of lightning**, the **rhodopsin can be activated**.

At no stimulus, the **photoreceptor** has a somewhat high $Ca^{2+}$ and $Na^+$ concentration, which means, that the **membrane potential is quite high**. 

![Screenshot 2026-10-05 at 08.58.53](../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-05%20at%2008.58.53.png)

During stimulus, the rhodopsin is activated, **activating the $G_t$ protein**. The $\alpha$-subunit of $G_t$ then **activates cGMP phosphodiesterase (PDE6)**, starting the synthesis of **GMP** from **cGMP**. This **fall in cGMP** cause the cation channels to close, such that the **k-leak channels** lower the membrane potential - resulting in a **hyper polarization**.

![Screenshot 2026-10-05 at 09.02.12](../z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-10-05%20at%2009.02.12.png)

The especially interesting part about this pathway is the sensitivity to photons, as the signal is **amplified** multiple times
- **One photon** activates **one rhodopsin** molecule
- **500** $G_t$ are activated
- **500** PDE6 are activated
- $10^6$ GMP are synthesizes
- **250** cation channels are closed
- $10^6$-$10^7$ $Na^+$ ions are prevented from entering for **one** second
- **1 mV** lowering of the membrane potential
