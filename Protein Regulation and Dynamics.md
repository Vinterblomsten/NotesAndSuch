Most proteins and enzymes do not work continuously at full speed, but are regulated in a coordinated fashion. This regulation occurs at many levels:
- Gene expression control - regulates amount of protein produced
- Degredation of protein regulation
- Confinement of specific proteins to specific cellular compartments
- Rapid adjustment of the protein activity it self

Enzymes usually have **regulatory sites** to which another molecules than the substrate binds, altering the rate at which the enzyme converts its substrate the product. There is often connections between substrates and regulatory molecules in a specific pathway, such as **feedback inhibition**, where the final product of a pathway doubles as an inhibitor of a previous enzyme, meaning that an abundance of final product slows the production of precursor substrate. Feedback inhibition is a form of **negative regulation**, but enzymes also has the property of binding to molecules, that speeds up the catalysis - **positive regulation**.

#### Allosteric Enzymes
Regulation of enzymes is called **allostery** - named after the greek for "other change". Many enzymes have at least two sites of binding, the **active site** and the **regulatory site**. When ligand binds to a regulatory site it goes trough a **conformational change** which slightly changes the structure. This can make the active site of the protein more or less accommodating of the substrate. 

Proteins with the ability to adopt two or more slightly different conformations, which regulates activity are called **allosteric**. This is not only enzymes, but many other proteins as well. Without the regulatory ligand, the protein will spontaneously interconvert between the two conformations (not necessarily 50/50), but with the regulatory ligand present, the enzyme will prefer the conformation, that fits it best, deeming the regulated conformation overwhelmingly abundant.

#### Phosphorylation
Another method of regulation involves binding a phosphate group to a side chain of the protein. The double negative charge of the phosphate can cause conformational change. This structural shift can affect the ability to bind ligands elsewhere. This use of **reversible protein phosphorylation** is abundant - about $1/3$ of the 10.000 proteins in the cell are phosphorylated at any one time. This system is connected to the states of the cell, for example controlling the division cycle.

It involves an enzyme-catalyzed transfer of the terminal phosphate group of $ATP$ to the hydroxyl group of the following aminos:
![[Screenshot 2026-08-24 at 11.01.51.png|432]]
The reaction is catalyzed by **protein kinase**. The reverse reaction called **dephosphorylation** is catalyzed by **protein phosphatase**. The phosphorylation can both inhibit or stimulate protein activity, depending on the protein. Hundreds of different protein kinases are responible for phosphorylating different proteins or sets of proteins, while a smaller set of protein phosphatase are responsible for the reverse reaction. Thus the phosphorylation is dependent on the activity of these proteins, which themselves are regulated in ways.

For many enzymes, this phosphorylation and dephosphorylation happens in a swift cycle, which, even though it takes energy in the form of $ATP$, allows rapid regulation. 

##### Covalent Modifications
Many temporary protein alterations with covalent bindings are utilized in the cell. More than 100 of these modifications takes place in the cell by enzyme catalysis, some of the important are:
- Phosphorylation can also create **docking sites** for binding both other proteins, promoting complexes. 
- Many proteins are modified by the addition of **acetyl** to **lysine** side chains, including histones. 
- The addition of **palmitate fatty acid** to a **cysteine** side chain drives protein to associate with cell membranes. 
- Attachment of **ubiquitin** (76-amino acid polypeptide) targets a protein for degradation.

Many proteins have **many sites of covalent modifications**, for example the protein **p53**, which plays a role in responding to DNA damage, has 20 sites of modification - allowing an enormous variety of combinations, which gives great flexibility for the behavior of the protein. Covalent modifications allows the cell to make optimal use of proteins, and respond rapidly to changes in the environment. 

##### GTP-Binding Proteins
Instead of enzymatically transfered ATP, these proteins transfer phosphate as part of **guinine nucleotide** (Guanosine triphosphate), which is bound to the protein in its active conformation, **hydrolyses a phosphate to switch to inactive**, and then dissociates the GDP in a cycle. 
![[Screenshot 2026-08-24 at 12.05.20.png|332]]
These GDP-binding proteins act as **molecular switches**, which activates in response to cell signals, and then binds to other proteins to regulate their activity.

##### Motor Proteins
A group of proteins utilize their range of conformations to drive movement. This is responsible for, among other things:
- Muscle contractions
- Moving organelles such as chromosomes during mitosis
- Moving macromolecules among cytoskeletal tracks

The series of conformation these proteins undergo, must bo directional, as non regulated random conformational shift would result in average standstill. This is done using **ATP-hydrolysis** to make some **steps irreversible**, as the energy for the reverse conformational change is too large to happen spontaneously. The result being random conformational shifts and thereby movement on a small scale, but irreversible steps making the long-term process directional.

##### Large Protein Machines
Many tasks in the cell is catalyzed by a highly coordinated, linked set og many proteins called a **protein machine**. For most of these machines, the hydrolysis of nucleoside triphosphates (ATP and GTP) drives an ordered series of conformational changes in some of the individual protein subunits, enabling an ensemble of coordinated movement - for example:
- Synthesis of proteins in ribosomes
- Replication and reperation of DNA

These protein machines aren't rapidly produces spontaneously. Instead special **scaffold proteins** are used to catalyze this proces by binding to the proteins needed with its structure domains, while the unstructured regions are flexible, so that they collide in ways that result in the resulting protein complexes. This allows the cell to deploy scaffold proteins in the specific regions at the right time, and thus controll the production of protein machines. Some other scaffolds are not proteins but RNA scaffolds. 

##### Intracellular Condensate
Aggregates of proteins, RNAs and protein machinces, can become huge, creating distinct biochemical compartments in the cell without any membranes. This occurs when the **macromolecule-macromolecule interaction** becomes strong enough, and a **phase seperation** occurs. This produces enclosed regions with a special biochemical environment. The largest of these is the **nucleolus**, which is the "ribosome factory" where ribosomal RNAs are transcribed and ribosomal subunits are formed. 

Some intrecellular condensates are based on amyloid structures, reversible assemblies of $\beta$ sheets, that coems together to produce a **hydrogel** that pulls other molecules into the condensate.
