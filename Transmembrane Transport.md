# Transmembrane Transport

Tags: #CellBiology #Physiology 

The plasma membrane controls all transport into and out of the cell, almost exclusively through **simple diffusion**, with the help of **transmembrane proteins** or through **vesicles fusion** (see [Compartments and Protein Distribution](Compartments%20and%20Protein%20Distribution.md)). 

##### Gradients of movement
Given the second law of thermodynamics, the molecules wants to spontaneously spread out as much as possible to decrease the order of the system. When looking at at divided system they wants to balance the concentrations meaning that the molecules moves towards the lowest concentration creating the **concentration gradient**. When combined with the [electrical properties](Electrical%20Properties%20%28NeuroFys%29.md) of charged particles, this creates the **[electrochemical gradient](Membrane%20Potential%20%28NeuroFys%29.md)**. 

### Simple diffusion
The **permeability** of a membran without membrane proteins is what gives rise to **simple diffusion**. This is when molecules moves through the lipid bilayer directly. The rates of this happening is dependent on the **size** and **polarity** of the molecules.
1. **Small non-polar** molecules dissolves readily in the lipid bilayer, and thus **diffuse easily** across the membrane - This include $O_2$ (32 Dalton), $CO_2$ (44 Dalton) and Steroid hormones
2. **Small uncharged** molecules does not dissolve in the lipid, but can sometimes diffuse across the bilayer - This includes $H_2O$ (18 Dalton) and ethanol (46 Dalton)
3. **Larger uncharged** molecules almost never diffuse across the membrane - This includes glucose (180 Daltons), amino acids and nucleosides
4. For **charged** molecules, practically no diffusion takes place - The membrane is $10^9$ times more permeable to water than small ions ($Na^+$ and $K^+$)

### Membrane Transport Proteins
The proteins used for transporting molecules across the lipid bilayer can be divided into two groups:

![540](z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-09-07%20at%2014.19.08.png)
#### Channels
Channels creates most of the **permeability** of the membrane, allowing the **influx** and **efflux** of specific molecules. This is the simplest mechanism of movement, as it is simply to create a hydrophilic "hole" in the hydrophobic wall that is the lipid bilayer.

All channels mediate **passive transport** - the molecules follow their **electrochemical gradient** whether this is in or out of the membrane.

Some of the simplest channels is the aquaporins, the makes the membrane permanently much more permeable to water. 
![Screenshot 2026-09-07 at 15.08.17](z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-09-07%20at%2015.08.17.png)
These aquaporins are also responsible for the **osmosis** of the cell. The net flux of water is from the area of lowest concentration of solutes to the highest. If the inside concentration - or **osmolarity**, exceeds the extracellular solution, it creates an influx of water molecules, which means that the **intracellular pressure increases**. To combat this, and reach **osmotic equilibrium**, ion pumps, such as the $Na^+/K^+$-ATPase, removes solutes from the cell.

**Gated Channels**
Som channels have multiple conformations which involves a gate, and can thus be open or closed by specific triggers. These gates are often specified by two things
- **The specification** of the molecules that can pass the gate. This varies from a single type of ion, to large gates that allow everything up to macro molecules to pass (Such as the toxin aerolysin). In human cells, and especially neurons, **ion channels are most common**.
- **The trigger** necessary for changing the conformation and opening/closing the gate.
	- **Mechanically-gated channels** respons to mechanical forces such as extracellular objets or auditory waves![Screenshot 2026-09-07 at 15.34.36](z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-09-07%20at%2015.34.36.png)
	- **Ligand-gated channels** can respond to either extracellular or intracellular molecules (ligands)
	- **Voltage-gated channels** are present in all cells but especially in the [neurons](Membrane%20Potential%20%28NeuroFys%29.md) of the nervous system
	- pH-gated channels
	- Phosphorylation-gated channels

![544](z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-09-07%20at%2015.27.12.png)

See [Action Potential (NeuroFys)](Action%20Potential%20%28NeuroFys%29.md) and [Membrane Potential (NeuroFys)](Membrane%20Potential%20%28NeuroFys%29.md) for more details on voltage gated channels.

#### Transporters
Transporters move specific molecules in/out of the compartment. A tranporter is differentiable from the channels by the property of **changing shape** in order to transport molecules. 

![348](z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-09-07%20at%2015.13.34.png)

##### Passive tranporters
Most transporters are passive transporters, meaning that they move molecules along their electrochemical gradient. An example of theis is the **glucose transporter** which by randomly changing between 12 conformations binds and releases glucose, such that on average it moves **towards its concentration gradient** (glucose doesn't have an electrical gradient).
![658](z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-09-07%20at%2014.24.43.png)
The **advantage of passive transporters over channels,** is the specificity necessary for larger molecules - for example the glucose transporter can only tranpose the specific isomer L-Glucose and not D-Glucose. The **disadvantage** is of course the speed of the proces. Some ion channels allow milions of ions to pass each second, which is **1000 times faster, than the fastes known transporter.**

##### Active transporters (Pumps)
Transporters can also move molecules against their electrochemical, in which case they **require energy** (ATP, coupled transport, etc.) - E.g. $Na^+/K^+$-pump

This is done using **ATP-driven pumps** and **Gradient-driven pumps** (coupled transporters) and **Light-driven pumps**

**Light-driven pumps** 
Uses the electromagnetic energy of the photon to transport $H^+$ out of the cell (**only in microorganism**)

**ATP-driven pumps**
Multiple ATP-driven pumps is present in the cell, but by far the most abundant is the **$Na^+/K^+$-ATPas**e. Other pumps include
- The $Ca^+$ pump, that transports $Ca^+$ out of the cell
- The $H^+$ pump the creates the **acidic environment** of the **lysozymes**.
- In plants, bacteria and funghi a different type $H^+$ pump

Sodium-Potassium Pump or **$Na^+/K^+$-ATPasen** creates the $Na^+$ and $K^+$ gradients
- It uses $\approx 30\%$ **of the ATP** in an average animal cell
- When 3 sodium binds to the protein, it changes the conformation given the electrical charge
- This hydrolyzes the ATP, adding a phosphate with a high-energy linkage
- This creates conformational change, that allows the sodium to be released and 2 potassium to bind
- The protein is then dephosphorilized and returns to the original conformation, releasing the phosphate

**Gradient-driven pumps**
Coupled transporters moves molecules (not just ions) **against their gradients** via the coupled molecules being moved towards their gradient
![475](z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-09-03%20at%2011.54.39.png)
This is used for
- Capture of nutrients
- Release of metabolites
- Recapture of neurotransmitters

The co-transport can be a **symporter** if the molecules moves in the same direction
- An example is the **$Na^+$-coupled serotonin transporter**, which drives the **recapture** of serotonin by a simultaneous intake of $Na^+$. 
	- Antidepressants block this transporter, keeping the serotonin in the synaptic cleft.
- Glucose-$Na^+$ symporters of the epithelial cells of the gut
![Screenshot 2026-09-07 at 15.04.54](z-Figures/Screenshot%202026-09-07%20at%2015.04.54.png)

The co-transporters can also be **antiporters**, where they first move an ion towards its gradient, and afterwards an ion **against** its gradient
